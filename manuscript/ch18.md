# Chapter Eighteen

Reyes came by the next afternoon, the way she'd said she would. She sat at our kitchen table and took my statement, and my dad's, and then she put her pen down and told me what she thought of the previous night.

It took about four minutes. She didn't raise her voice once. It was the worst four minutes of my life, and that includes the morning on the cliff stairs.

When she was done, she picked the pen back up.

"Also," she said, "it was good work."

My dad, at the counter, made a sound that might have been a laugh.

"Both of those things are true," Reyes said. "I don't need you to pick one." She looked at me. "Your friend Des says that a lot, apparently."

"He does," I said.

"He's right."

* * *

The rest came in over the next two weeks, the way the end of a case comes in. Not all at once. Piece by piece, like the tide.

The swab from the railing came back from Portland. The blood was Nora's.

The shoe in the Kessler's bag had Camille's DNA inside it, and rust on the toe that matched the railing, and two of Nora's fingerprints on the heel, right where you'd grab somebody's foot if they were falling.

The carrier records for the number ending in 4417 showed every text between that phone and Theo's, going back eleven months. Reyes didn't tell me what was in them. I didn't ask. The prepaid cards that kept the number running had been bought at Harbor Foods, four of them, with a rewards card registered to Nora Petrakis, because Nora never once in her life passed up the chance to save forty cents on a gallon of gas.

The phone itself was in the console of the green wagon, under a packet of tissues. Dead.

The medical examiner revised her report. She said Camille's injuries were consistent with death within moments of the fall. Reyes told me that one in person, sitting on our porch steps, with her coffee going cold beside her.

"It means she probably couldn't have been saved," Reyes said. "Even if Nora had called the second it happened."

I thought about that. "But she didn't know that."

"No," Reyes said. "She didn't know that. She didn't go down there and find out. She didn't call anybody who could have. She sat in her garage for two hours deciding which version to believe." She turned her mug in her hands. "The ME's report doesn't change what Nora did. It just changes what Camille went through. And I'm glad, for her family, that it was fast. That's all it means."

* * *

Nora Petrakis was formally charged eleven days after the night at the point.

It took that long for the county to sort out everything she'd done, because it turned out to be a lot of things, and none of them was the one thing the town had been whispering about. She hadn't pushed Camille. The evidence agreed with her about that. The shoe in her hand, the angle of the fall, the broken stone where Camille's heel had caught, all of it.

The DA's statement was two pages long. Priya posted the whole thing on the Beacon without changing a word, with a note at the top that said this was the last thing she would ever post about Nora Petrakis.

Sexual abuse of a minor. Multiple counts. That was first, and it was the longest part. Then tampering with physical evidence, for the shoe. Tampering with a witness, for the 5:47 text and everything after, for telling a seventeen-year-old what to say and making him afraid not to say it. Burglary, for the afternoon of the funeral, for walking into our house through a garage door my sister had named after her. Hindering prosecution. Obstruction. And a line at the end that said the DA's office was still reviewing the circumstances of the death itself and that additional charges were possible.

"Is it enough?" I asked Reyes.

She thought about it for a long time. She always does. I like that about her.

"It's a lot," she said. "It's going to be a long time before she's anywhere but a courtroom or a cell." She looked out at our street. "Is it enough? I don't think anything's enough. That's not my department. My department is making sure it's true."

* * *

Sheriff Lockhart gave a second press conference. It was shorter than the first one. Thirty-two seconds. I timed it.

"New evidence came to light," he said, on the courthouse steps, in front of the same cameras. Like evidence has legs and walks into a department on its own, instead of being dragged in by a detective he'd spent a month trying to stop, and a gas station owner, and a hardware store clerk, and a girl dressed as a strawberry, and two juniors and a senior sitting on the floor of a school archive.

He thanked "the hard work of this department." He didn't say Reyes's name. He didn't take any questions.

Reyes stood at the very back of the crowd, in her gray rain jacket, holding a coffee she wasn't drinking. When it was over, she caught me looking at her and shrugged.

Later, on our porch, she told me something I've thought about a lot since.

"He asked me for the whole file," she said. "The day after. Everything. Every note, every photo, every interview." She frowned at her mug. "Which is normal. He's the sheriff. But he asked twice if there was anything in it about your sister. About Rowan. He wanted to know if Nora said anything about that summer."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him it was all in the file." She looked at me sideways. "It is. Mostly."

I didn't ask what *mostly* meant. I thought I knew. Nora on the bench, saying *she asked me if I could keep a secret, if it wasn't hers*. I'd told Reyes that part on the walk back to the truck that night. I'd watched her decide what to do with it.

"Why did he want to know?" I asked.

"I've been here fifteen months," Reyes said. "I've learned that in Quiet Hollow, it's never just one reason." She finished her coffee, cold, in one swallow, and made a face. "Go do your homework, Wren."

* * *

The town did what towns do. It talked about it nonstop for exactly as long as it was new, and then it quietly filed it on the shelf of things you don't bring up at barbecues. The same shelf my sister has been sitting on for six years.

Everybody knew about Theo within two days. Nobody released anything to a bunch of high schoolers. But news moves through the servers faster than any official announcement, and by that Friday half the school knew why a grown woman had been arrested and what she had to do with Theo Marsh.

And they got it wrong. That's the part that made me angriest. Not the gossip. The words.

*Theo's affair.* *Theo was hooking up with some old lady.* *Did you know Theo was sleeping with Camille's babysitter, that's so messed up.* Like it was something he did. Like he'd gone looking for it.

Kenzie Ochoa said *ew, Theo* in the hallway, loud, and I stopped walking.

"He was sixteen," I said.

She blinked at me. "I know, that's why it's so—"

"No. Listen. He was sixteen when it started. She was forty-seven. She was in his house every day taking care of his grandma. That's not him doing something gross, Kenzie. That's something somebody did *to* him."

Kenzie looked at me for a long time. Her face changed.

"Oh," she said quietly. "I didn't think about it like that."

"Nobody does," I said. "That's the problem."

She thought about it. Then she went and said it to the girls she'd been talking to. I heard her, down the hall. *No, actually, think about it.* Kenzie isn't a deep person. But she's a person who notices when a dot isn't moving, and then says so out loud.

It doesn't mean Theo did nothing. He lied. He let Camille's family believe she'd gone up there alone. He helped build an alibi out of captions and kept his mouth shut for three weeks while a whole town called his girlfriend's death an accident. The DA's office decided not to charge him with anything, after his statement, and after what his new lawyer called *the full context of his situation*. But not being charged isn't the same as not having done anything. I think Theo knows that better than anybody. I think he's going to know it for a long time.

He didn't come to school for most of that week. When he came back, he was quieter. People left him alone, in a way that was different from before. Less careful. More like they didn't know what to say, which was honest, at least.

He found me at my locker on the Thursday. He stood there with his hands in his hoodie pocket, knee brace on, looking at the floor.

"Priya said you figured out the time," he said. "The 5:47."

"Yeah."

"I looked at that text every day for three weeks. Every day. And I never—" He shook his head. "I never once thought about what time it was."

"You weren't supposed to," I said. "That's sort of the point. She knew you wouldn't."

He was quiet for a while.

"Do you think Cam hated me?" he said. "At the end?"

I'd known he was going to ask. Priya told me he'd asked her too, and she'd said she didn't know. I'd been thinking about it ever since.

"She texted you not to come," I said. "And when you came anyway, she told you to go back. Over and over. Even though she was furious." I looked at him. "I think she was trying to keep you out of it. I think she was going up there to end it for you."

Theo didn't say anything. His jaw worked. He nodded, once, at the floor.

Then he limped off down the hallway, and I watched him go, the right leg swinging out stiff on every step. I'd watched that walk on a screen eleven times. This was the first time I'd watched it and seen a kid.

* * *

I found Priya in the library on a Tuesday, at the leaky table, laptop open. She was working on something with the specific focus she gets when a piece actually matters.

"What are you writing?"

"The thing I said I wanted to write. Before any of this." She turned the screen so I could see.

*Who Camille Malhotra Actually Was.*

Underneath it was a draft full of small, specific, unglamorous details. The group project fight. Her relay splits. The crushed heels. The poem about the ocean. The way she reread every text twice before she sent it. A thing about ranch dressing that three different people had told Priya about, separately, without being asked. Like it mattered more than anything else they could say.

"I'm not putting any of the other part in," Priya said. "Not Nora. Not Theo. Not the point. She doesn't need to be a true crime footnote in her own memorial. She gets to just be a person who put ranch on everything, including pizza. Which is deranged, for the record. But it was hers."

"That's a good piece."

"I know it's a good piece. I've been working on it for two weeks." She looked up at me. There was something easier in her face than there'd been since the vigil. Not healed. Maybe never healed. But easier. "I'm not saying we're friends now. I don't even know what we are. Given how we met and everything since."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair." Almost a smile. "But I don't hate you anymore. I want that on the record, since apparently everybody in your life has to say things out loud to you or you assume the worst."

"I do assume the worst."

"You really do. It's kind of your whole thing." She looked back at her screen. "For what it's worth, I don't think you were only ever using Camille to get back to your sister. I think both were true the whole time, like Des said, and I was too angry that week to let them both be true at once. I've had two weeks to sit with it." She paused. "I still think you owe me honesty about which one wins when they don't line up. But that's not today's conversation."

"No," I said. "Not today's."

"And Wren." She didn't look up. "When you start on Rowan. For real. I want in."

I didn't know what to say.

"I'm good at archives," Priya said. "I'm good at records. And you're going to need somebody who doesn't let you keep them on need-to-know." She typed a sentence, deleted it, typed it again. "Also, Camille died thinking something happened to your sister that nobody ever answered. I'd like to answer it. For her."

I sat down across from her. We didn't say much after that. Just two people sharing a leaky table in a library, which we hadn't managed once since this whole thing started. It felt like something small and real getting rebuilt, one ordinary Tuesday at a time.

* * *

Des found me at my locker after. He leaned against the one next to it like he'd been standing there for hours instead of the four seconds it actually took him to get there.

"So," he said. "We solved a mur—"

"Don't."

"—a *case*. We solved a case. I was going to say case."

"You were going to say murder."

"I was going to say murder," he admitted. "And then my brain caught up and realized it wasn't one. Which is somehow worse? I don't know what to call it."

I thought about that. I'd been thinking about it for two weeks.

"It's the wrong kind," I said.

He looked at me. "Of what?"

"Of anything. It's not a murder and it's not an accident. Somebody didn't push her, and somebody also didn't save her. And then lied about it to everybody, including a kid." I closed my locker. "There's not a word for that. It's just the wrong kind."

Des was quiet for a while, walking beside me down the hall. Backpack half zipped. Hair sticking up in the back.

"You okay?" he said. "For real. Not the *fine* you say to everybody including yourself."

I thought about it honestly, which took a second, because I hadn't actually asked myself that in a long time.

"I don't know yet," I said. "I solved it. Start to finish. And it doesn't feel like I thought it would. Camille's still gone. Priya's still sad. Nora's still a person who really did love her, and it didn't stop her from anything." I shoved my hands in my pockets. "Nothing closes as clean as it's supposed to."

"Yeah. I don't think it's supposed to." He bumped my shoulder with his. The closest thing to a hug either of us does on a school day. "That's what actually solving something is like. As opposed to how it looks on TV."

"When did you get wise?"

"Twenty-six minutes, staring at a dot," Des said. "It aged me. I'm basically eighty now."

We walked out into the parking lot. It was cold and bright, the kind of November day where the sky is so clean it looks fake.

"For the record," Des said. "You did good. Scary. Reckless. Going to give me gray hair before graduation. But good."

"Thanks, Des."

"Don't get used to me being nice," he said. "It's a limited engagement."

Kenzie Ochoa ran past us toward the buses, her backpack bouncing. She stopped, turned around, and ran backward for a few steps.

"Wren!" she yelled. "I still have it! It's been almost a month!"

She held up her retainer case like a trophy.

"Proud of you, Kenzie," I said.

"I know," she said, and turned and ran.

* * *

My dad made an actual dinner that night. Not the standing-at-the-counter kind. Roast chicken, potatoes, green beans from a can because neither of us is a hero. We ate at the table across from each other without either of us performing normal for the other one. I think it might have been the first time in six years that had happened.

He didn't ask about the case. I didn't bring up the photo. But when I got up to clear my plate, he caught my wrist, just for a second.

"I'm proud of you," he said. "I don't say that enough either."

He let go before either of us had to figure out what to do with how much that landed.

After dinner I found him in the front hall with a dish towel and a bottle of glass cleaner, standing in front of Rowan's picture. The green dress. The laugh at something off-camera.

He'd taken it down off the wall. I'd never seen it off the wall. There was a rectangle on the paint behind it, a shade lighter than the rest.

"I do it on Sundays," he said, without looking at me. "In case you were wondering. When you're at your run. Every Sunday."

"I know," I said. "I figured it out when I was eleven."

He almost smiled. He held the frame out to me.

I took it. I held my sister in my hands for the first time in six years. Not a phone. Not a screenshot. Her. I sprayed the glass and wiped it the way he showed me, in circles, then straight lines to get the streaks out, and handed it back.

He hung it up. He took a step back and tilted it a hair to the left, then a hair to the right, then left again.

"Dad," I said. "The photo. Under her bed. The torn one."

He went still. He kept looking at Rowan.

"I'm not going to ask tonight," I said. "I just want to say I'm going to ask. Sometime. And I want you to know I'm going to ask."

He didn't say anything for a long time. The kitchen clock ticked, four minutes fast.

"I'm not going to tell you I don't know," he said finally. "Not again. That's the only thing I can promise you right now." He folded the dish towel over his shoulder. "When you ask, I'm going to tell you something true. Even if it's *not yet*."

"Okay."

"Is that enough?"

I thought about Reyes on our porch steps. *I don't think anything's enough.*

"It's a lot," I said.

* * *

I went upstairs.

There's a drawer in my desk where I keep the birthday cards from my mother. Every few years, a different postmark. I've never opened one.

I opened the drawer and looked at them. A small stack of envelopes, my name on the front in handwriting I don't really remember.

I didn't open them. I want to be honest about that. I'm not there yet, and I'm not sure I'll ever be, and I'm not going to pretend some big thing happened that didn't.

But I looked at them. And I thought about the theory I've been running since I was ten. That people who look back at everything aren't the kind of people who leave.

I've looked back at a lot of things in the last six weeks. I've looked back at things I'd been keeping at almost two years for a reason. I went into the room I'd been staying out of. I asked the question. I followed it all the way to a bench at the edge of a cliff.

I don't think my mother ever did any of that.

I closed the drawer. Gently. I didn't throw anything away.

* * *

The box was still under the bed, against the wall, where my dad had pushed it. I pulled it out and sat on the floor and took out two pictures.

The first one was the Fourth of July. Rowan in the brand-new jacket hanging off her shoulders like a cape. *hand-me-down day!! 🔥 finally warm.* Nora's arm at the edge of the frame, holding a paper plate, in the twin of the same coat.

That one I understood now. All the way.

The second one was the torn one.

Rowan, laughing, later that summer. The jacket softer, the collar flipped up. Her arm slung around somebody tall. And beside her, that ragged careful gap where a person used to be.

It had nothing to do with Nora. Nothing to do with Camille, or Theo, or a coat with a paper trail six years long. Whoever had been standing there was part of a completely different story. One tangled up in my sister's last summer in a way I hadn't even started to pull apart.

Here's what I knew, sitting there.

There were two jackets. One of them was in an evidence bag at the county now, the one Camille died in. And the other one, the one on the poster, the one Rowan was wearing the night she walked off the bonfire beach, has never been found. Not on the rocks. Not in a closet. Not anywhere. Wherever my sister went, it went with her.

It's still out there. Or it's still somewhere. Things don't just disappear. Not really. That's the first thing you learn finding lost things. Things are always somewhere. It's just a question of who's been keeping them there.

I knew that the week before she disappeared, my sister sat in a babysitter's kitchen and asked if somebody could keep a secret that wasn't hers. And then decided not to tell it.

I knew that somebody left one word under her last post, two years ago, from an account with no name. *Still.*

I knew that the sheriff of Quiet Hollow had asked twice whether there was anything in Camille's file about my sister.

And I knew that my father had looked at this hole in a photograph and said *I don't know* in the only voice I've never once heard him use for the truth.

I'd spent six years being told I was too young. Too close. Too much. That it wasn't my case.

I'd just solved one that wasn't mine either.

I took my phone out and opened the app that isn't really a notebook. I made a new channel. I typed the name and looked at it for a long time before I hit enter.

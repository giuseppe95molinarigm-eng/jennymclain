# Chapter Seven

By Monday morning I'd slept about three hours, and I'd watched the 4:58 AM car eleven times on my phone.

It doesn't look like much. That's the worst part. Headlights in the fog, coming from the direction of town. One bright, one dim, like a face with one eye half closed. Then the shape of a small dark car going past the pumps toward the point. Then nothing for twenty-five minutes. Then the same headlights coming back the other way, going a little faster.

Somebody went out to Hollow Point in the dark, the morning after a girl went off it, and came home before anybody knew.

I kept trying to think of a normal reason. A fisherman. Somebody's early shift at the lighthouse, except nobody works at the lighthouse, it's been automated since the nineties. A teenager sneaking home from somewhere. A person who couldn't sleep and liked to drive.

Every normal reason had the same problem. Whoever it was had already been out there once, at 11:26 the night before, and left at 12:16. That's not somebody who likes to drive. That's somebody who forgot something.

I thought about the shoe. The left one sitting upright outside the tape. The right one gone.

I was still thinking about it when I got to my locker and found Priya Malhotra waiting for me.

* * *

She was standing with her back against the locker next to mine, arms crossed. Ponytail back. Not a single strand out of place. She had the same expression she'd had on the cliff on Wednesday, telling me her cousin wasn't going to be my little project.

My first thought was not hope. My first thought was: great, round two.

"I'm not here to fight," she said, before I could open my mouth.

"Okay."

"I mean it. I had a whole weekend to decide whether I still hate you for Wednesday. And I do, a little. But that's not why I'm here."

I opened my locker slower than I needed to, mostly to give myself a second. "Then why are you here?"

She looked up and down the hallway before she answered. That told me more than anything she'd said. Priya doesn't check who's listening. Priya is the one everyone else checks for, because if something matters she'll have it posted on the Beacon by lunch. Her checking meant this wasn't going on the Beacon. Not yet.

"I've been working on a piece," she said. "About Camille. Not how she died. Who she was. Before everybody turned her into a candle on a cliff." Her jaw tightened. "I wanted one honest thing about her out there that isn't a slideshow with a sad song."

"That's a good idea."

"I didn't ask what you thought." But it came out with no heat. More like a reflex. "I went to the library Friday, after the counselors cleared out. To ask Ms. Feiler for the old relay team photo from freshman year. The good one, where Camille's actually laughing, instead of her school picture where she looks like a hostage."

"Okay."

"And Ms. Feiler said something. Kind of offhand, while she was looking for it." Priya's voice stayed level, but I watched her fingers tighten on the strap of her bag. "She said Camille had been in the archive room a few weeks ago. By herself. Reading old newspapers."

I waited.

"From six years ago," Priya said. "The week your sister went missing."

There are sentences that change the shape of a day. That one changed the shape of about four days at once, all of them still on their way.

"Say that again," I said. It's a stupid thing to say when you heard somebody perfectly the first time. My mouth needed something to do while the rest of me caught up.

"Camille. My cousin Camille, who I would bet actual money never once said your sister's name to me in my entire life. She went into the archive and read about Rowan Ashby." Priya's voice cracked, just a little, on Rowan's name. It was the first uneven thing she'd let me see. "Ms. Feiler said she didn't think anything of it at the time. Kids pull weird stuff for projects. But Camille didn't take notes. She just sat there for like forty minutes, reading, and then she left, and she didn't say why."

"When? What week?"

"She didn't remember exactly. End of September. She said she'd have to look at the sign-in sheet." Priya frowned at me. "Why does the week matter?"

It mattered because it would tell me how long Camille had been carrying whatever she'd found before she walked up the road on Monday night in my sister's jacket.

I didn't say that. Not yet. There's a version of me that would have, the version that talks first and regrets it during fourth period. But something in Priya's face stopped me. She looked like someone holding out a piece of broken glass, not sure yet whether it was going to cut her.

"I don't know yet," I said. That was mostly true. "But I don't think it's nothing."

"I didn't think it was nothing either. That's the problem." She shifted her bag on her shoulder. "I spent all weekend trying on boring explanations. A history project. A true crime thing, people do that, they read about old local cases because it's creepy. I've tried like six boring explanations, and none of them fit."

"So you came to me."

"I came to you because apparently you're the only person in this town who has spent six years becoming an expert on one specific missing girl." She said it flat, no apology. I respected that more than I would have if she'd tried to soften it. "And I needed to know if this actually means something, or if I'm about to hand my family's private business to the person with the worst reputation in school for turning other people's tragedies into projects. So. Does it mean something?"

I thought about lying to her. Not a big lie. Something small to buy time, so I could work it alone the way I do everything. But she'd walked over here first. She'd handed me the piece of glass first. There's a kind of person who does that, and a kind who doesn't, and I wanted, for once, to be the kind who didn't waste it.

"Camille was wearing a jacket when she died," I said. "Navy. Kind of old. Plaid lining."

Priya's eyes narrowed. "I know what she was wearing. I've seen her in it a hundred times. She wore it all summer."

"Do you know where she got it?"

"I don't know. Thrifted it? She thrifted everything. Or somebody gave it to her." Priya shook her head. "Camille had a lot of stuff people gave her. People just gave Camille things. Why?"

"Because my sister was wearing the exact same jacket the night she disappeared."

Priya didn't flinch. That surprised me. She just went very still, the way you go still when you've stepped on a stair that isn't there.

"That's a big thing to say to someone in a hallway before first period," she said.

"You asked."

"I did." She breathed out through her nose, long and slow, like she was letting go of an argument she'd been having with herself since Friday. "Okay. Okay. Here's where I am. I still think you'd chase this even if it were a stray dog and not my cousin. I haven't forgotten that. But I can't sit on the one piece of this that doesn't add up just because I don't like who's good at adding things up."

She pulled a folded index card out of her back pocket. The kind she uses for interview questions. The edges were already soft from being carried around all weekend.

"Ms. Feiler's exact words," she said. "As close as I could get them. I wrote them down in the parking lot. In case it matters later. In case somebody asks how we knew."

I took it. "We?"

"Don't make it weird." But it didn't sound like a no. It sounded like two people agreeing to stop making each other's lives worse. "I'm not doing this so we can be friends, Wren. I'm doing this because whatever my cousin was looking for in that archive, she didn't get to finish looking. And I'd like to know what it was before somebody buries it with her."

"I want the same thing."

"I know. That's the only reason I'm standing here." She hesitated. Then, quieter, like it cost her something: "My mom asked me last night why I was even talking to you. Half my family was at my aunt's house, doing funeral stuff, there are like four hundred casseroles in that kitchen. And I said—" She stopped.

"You said what?"

"I said Wren Ashby thinks Camille's jacket means something. Because somebody at the vigil told me you'd been asking Gary Ostrander about it, and the shoe, on Tuesday, before the van even left." She gave me a look. "Were you?"

I had been. Briefly. Gary didn't remember anything about the jacket except that it was "dark." I'd asked him three different ways.

"And it came out wrong," Priya went on. "It came out like I was mad about it. And my aunt kind of looked up, and then everybody looked up, and then my mom started a whole thing about how that Ashby girl needs to leave grieving families alone." She shrugged, jerky. "So. Everybody in that kitchen knows you're asking questions about Camille's jacket. I'm sorry. That's not great for whatever this is."

"Who was there?"

"Everybody. My mom. My aunt. My other aunties. Neighbors. Church people. Half the women in Quiet Hollow bring a dish and stay three hours." She looked at the clock over the trophy case. "Don't make me regret this, Wren."

She walked off toward homeroom before I could answer. That was probably smart. I didn't have a good answer ready.

* * *

I stood at my locker until the bell rang, turning the index card over in my hand.

Priya's handwriting is small and square, the handwriting of somebody who was praised for it in second grade and never got over it.

* * *

I found Des at lunch. He was already at our table with his laptop open and his lunch untouched, and he looked like he'd slept even less than I had.

"Okay," he said, before I'd even sat down. "Either I heard three different versions of a rumor that Priya Malhotra cornered you at your locker this morning, or you two are starting a podcast. I need to know which one, because both are insane for different reasons."

"Camille was researching Rowan. Three weeks before she died. In the archive."

Des put his fork down very carefully, the way he does when a piece of information is too big to hold with a hand that's also holding food.

"Okay," he said. "That's not a coincidence-shaped sentence."

"No."

"What was she looking for?"

I slid him Priya's index card. He read it. He read it again. He got to the underlined sentence and looked up at me.

"So what are you doing still sitting here?"

* * *

Ms. Feiler has run the Hollow Bay High library for longer than anyone can remember. She's small and silver-haired and wears cardigans with pockets full of things: pencils, cough drops, the little rubber thumb things librarians use to turn pages. She has a voice that never goes above a library volume, even in the parking lot. Even at football games. I've been coming to the library since freshman year, mostly to be somewhere nobody would talk to me. She's let me. That's the closest thing we have to a relationship.

She was behind the desk when I walked in, putting new barcodes on a stack of paperbacks. She looked up and saw me, and then she saw who was behind me.

Priya. I'd texted her from the lunch table. She'd come.

Ms. Feiler took off her reading glasses and folded them very slowly.

"I wondered how long it would take," she said. "Come in the back."

The archive is a long narrow room behind the library with no windows. It smells like dust and vinegar, which Ms. Feiler says is the microfilm slowly falling apart, and there's nothing anybody can do about it. Shelves of old yearbooks. Filing cabinets. Two microfilm readers that look like something out of an old spy movie. A sign-in sheet on a clipboard by the door, because Ms. Feiler believes in sign-in sheets the way some people believe in God.

She flipped back through it without being asked. She ran her finger down the column of names and stopped.

[[LOG]]
9/23 | 2:50 PM | C. Malhotra | Ledger, Aug. (6 yrs) + missing persons material
[[/LOG]]

Camille's handwriting. Round letters. A little circle instead of a dot over the i in Malhotra. Priya made a small noise and looked at the ceiling for a second.

"She asked for the same reel you've asked for twice," Ms. Feiler said to me. "Freshman year, and once last winter. You never stayed very long."

I hadn't known she'd noticed. Of course she'd noticed.

She got the reel out of a drawer, threaded it into the machine with quick, sure fingers, and switched it on. The screen glowed. She scrolled until she found it, and then she stepped back so we could see.

The Quiet Hollow Ledger. Six years ago, the third week of August. Front page.

LOCAL TEEN MISSING AFTER BEACH BONFIRE.

The photo was Rowan's last post. Somebody at the paper had pulled it right off her account and cropped out everyone else, so it was just my sister, mid-laugh, collar up, the fire behind her.

In the second column, the paragraph I knew was coming.

*Ashby was last seen wearing a navy canvas work jacket with a distinctive red-and-black plaid lining, jean shorts, and white sneakers. Anyone with information is asked to contact the Quiet Hollow Sheriff's Department.*

Below that, a quote from Sheriff Boone Lockhart. *We are pursuing every avenue. We have every reason to believe Rowan will be home soon.*

I'd read that quote before. It never stops being a strange thing to read.

Priya was leaning close to the screen. I watched her read the paragraph about the jacket. I watched her read it again. I watched the moment it landed, which was not the same moment as reading it.

"That's her jacket," she said. Not loud. "That's Camille's jacket. That's what she was wearing in all her pictures this summer."

"I know."

"You said that this morning, and I believed you, but I didn't—" She stopped. She put two fingers on the glass of the reader, right on the word *plaid*. "Camille saw this."

"Yes," Ms. Feiler said quietly. "She did."

Then she reached past us and scrolled to the next frame. A photo of a telephone pole on Fir Street with a poster stapled to it. The same poster at the bottom of my shoebox. My dad's plain font. *LAST SEEN WEARING.*

"She took a picture of this one," Ms. Feiler said. "With her phone. I remember because I almost told her that you aren't supposed to photograph the microfilm, and then I looked at her face, and I didn't."

"What was on her face?" I asked.

Ms. Feiler thought about it. She's someone who thinks before she answers, which is rarer than it should be.

"She looked like someone who'd just found the answer to a question she was hoping she'd gotten wrong."

* * *

"The question," Priya said. "On the card. You told me she asked you one question before she left."

Ms. Feiler looked at her for a long moment. Then at me. Then back at Priya.

"I told you I'd have to think about it. I've thought about it." She took her reading glasses out of her cardigan pocket and turned them over in her hands without putting them on. "I've been the librarian at this school for twenty-six years. I've learned that what people look up is their own business. But Camille is dead. And I've decided that matters more than her privacy does."

She put the glasses back in her pocket.

"She stood right there by the door with her bag on her shoulder. And she asked me if I remembered when Rowan Ashby went missing. I said I did. I said everybody did." Ms. Feiler's voice was very even. "And she asked me whether they ever found the jacket."

Nobody in the archive moved.

"I said no," Ms. Feiler said. "They never found anything. And she said *okay* in a very small voice, and she thanked me, and she left."

* * *

Priya and I didn't talk on the way out of the library. We didn't talk all the way down the hallway. We ended up sitting on the floor by the vending machines outside the gym, because it was the closest place nobody would be, and we sat there with our backs against the wall and the machines humming over our heads.

"She thought it was Rowan's," Priya said finally. "Didn't she. She was wearing it, and she found out it looked exactly like the jacket on the missing girl's poster, and she went and checked, and she asked if they ever found it. And they didn't." She swallowed. "She thought she was wearing your sister's jacket."

"I think so."

"Is she?" Priya turned to look at me. "Was she? Is it actually Rowan's?"

"I don't know." It was the truest thing I'd said all day. "If it is, somebody had it for six years. Somebody kept it. And then somebody gave it to Camille."

"And Camille figured that out."

"Camille figured out *something*. Three weeks before she died." I pulled my knees up. "And then she went up to a lighthouse she was scared of, in the middle of the night, to meet somebody, and she turned her location off so nobody would see where she went."

Priya was quiet for a long time.

"She asked me in September if I remembered Rowan Ashby," she said. "It was so random. We were in my car. She asked if I remembered her, and I said barely, I was ten, and she said *yeah, me too*, and changed the subject. I didn't think about it again until Friday." She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "I should have asked her. I should have asked her why."

"You couldn't have known."

"That's not the point." She dropped her hands. Her eyes were dry, and furious. "She was carrying this by herself. For three weeks. She didn't tell me. She didn't tell anybody. Why wouldn't she tell anybody?"

I didn't have an answer. I could only think of one reason you'd keep something like that to yourself, and it was that you were afraid of the person it led back to.

Or you were afraid nobody would believe you about them.

The bell rang for fifth period. Neither of us got up.

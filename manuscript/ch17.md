# Chapter Seventeen

Priya came with us. Nobody asked her to. She was just in the truck when my dad started it, sitting in the middle of the bench seat with her seatbelt already on and her face set like stone.

My dad looked at her.

"She was my cousin," Priya said.

He didn't argue. He put the truck in reverse.

Des stood in the driveway with his phone in his hand. He didn't try to come. He's the brakes. He knows the difference between being the brakes and being left behind.

"Keep your location on," he said through my window. "I'm watching your dot. I'm calling Reyes back right now and I'm staying on with her. If your dot stops moving for one second longer than it should—"

"It won't."

"If it does." He held up his phone, the map open, my little face on the screen at the top of our street. "I'm watching."

* * *

It was almost dark by the time we passed the Fuel Stop. Delgado was standing outside under the canopy by the pumps, not doing anything, just standing there with his arms crossed, looking down the road toward the point. He saw my dad's truck and lifted one hand. My dad lifted one back.

Then the road narrowed into the curves, and the trees closed in, and the fog started coming in off the water in long low sheets the way it does in November, and the Fuel Stop's lights disappeared behind us.

Nobody talked. My dad drove with both hands on the wheel. Priya stared straight ahead. I watched the dark go by and thought about Camille walking this road three and a half weeks ago, on foot, in the jacket, with her phone in her pocket, going to ask a question she thought she already knew the answer to.

The parking lot at Hollow Point is gravel, with room for maybe twelve cars. There was one.

A dark green wagon, parked crooked, nose toward the lighthouse. The engine was off. The driver's door was open. The dome light was on inside, lighting up an empty seat.

My dad parked behind it. He turned off the truck. He sat there a second.

"I'm going first," he said.

"Dad—"

"I'm going first, Wren." He opened his door. "That's not up for debate."

* * *

The lighthouse beam swept over us every eleven seconds. I counted without meaning to, the way I'd counted at the vigil. Eleven. The beam went out over the water and came back and lit the fog white and showed us everything for a second and then took it away.

The gravel path to the lighthouse. The grass on either side of it. The yellow tape at the top of the cliff stairs, sagging now, half torn by wind. Somebody's sunflowers, dead and brown, still tied to the broken railing.

And the bench.

There's a bench by the lighthouse door, facing the ocean. It has a plaque on it for somebody who loved this view. In the summer people eat ice cream on it.

Nora was sitting on it.

She wasn't near the railing. That was the first thing I checked, and the first thing my body let go of. She was nowhere near the edge. She was sitting on the bench in a long wool coat, with her hands in her lap and her reading glasses pushed up on her head, looking out at the water.

She looked like somebody waiting for a bus.

She heard our feet on the gravel and turned her head. The beam came around and lit her up. I watched her see my dad. Then me. Then Priya.

She didn't run. She didn't stand up.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Cal. I'm sorry. I thought it would be her. The detective."

"It will be," my dad said. "She's coming."

Nora nodded slowly. Like that was fine. Like that was what she'd expected.

"I've been coming out here every few nights," she said. "Did you know that? I sit right here. I don't go near the—" She didn't look at the railing. "I just sit. I tell her I'm sorry. I don't know if that's a thing you can do. I'm not a religious person. I keep doing it anyway."

The beam swept away. In the dark, I heard Priya breathe in, sharp, through her teeth.

* * *

My dad didn't sit. He stood about ten feet from the bench with his hands in his coat pockets, between Nora and the two of us. I don't think he even knew he was doing it. I think his body just put itself there.

"They searched your house," he said.

"I know. Theo stopped answering." She looked down at her hands. "When he stopped answering, I knew."

She turned her right hand over in her lap. The bandage was gone. There was a line across the middle of her palm, pink and new and shiny, the kind of scar that will go white in a year.

"Cheese grater," I said.

I hadn't planned to say anything. It just came out.

Nora looked up at me. For a second, something in her face almost smiled. The way you smile at a kid who's figured out the magic trick.

"You always were the smart one," she said. "Rowan used to say that. She'd say *the gremlin's going to be smarter than all of us.*" Her face crumpled, just for a second, and then she put it back. "Railing. It was the railing. The rusty part, where it's broken out. I reached through it in the dark and it cut me open like a can."

"To get the shoe," I said.

"To get the shoe."

* * *

I'm going to write down what she told us. All of it. In order, as best as I can remember it. I've thought a lot about whether I should, and I've decided Camille deserves to have it written down by somebody who was paying attention.

She started with the jackets.

"I bought two," she said. "I always buy two of anything I love. My mother did that. I picked them up a couple of weeks before the Fourth. I wore one to the beach, and I had the other one still in the bag in my car. And Rowan was standing by the fire in a tank top, shaking, with her lips going blue, because she was seventeen and seventeen-year-olds don't believe in weather. So I went and got the other one out of my car." She smiled, a little, at the water. "I put it around her shoulders. And she looked down at herself and then at me and said *Nora, we're twins*. And then she said it was a hand-me-down, as a joke, because I was so old. She wrote me a thank-you note. Can you imagine? A seventeen-year-old. A note."

"I know," I said. "They found it in your dresser."

Something flickered in her face. "I keep everything."

"The jacket she was wearing when she disappeared," I said. "That was yours. The one you gave her."

"It was hers by then." Nora's voice went very soft. "It was hers. And then she was gone, and it was gone, and every time for six years I saw a navy coat anywhere, at the grocery store, at the ferry, on some stranger in Portland, my heart would stop. Every time. I'd think, *there it is*. I'd think, *somebody has it, somebody knows*." She shook her head. "It was never hers."

* * *

"And Camille," Priya said.

It was the first thing she'd said. Her voice was perfectly flat.

Nora looked at her for a long time. In the dark between the beams, I couldn't see her face. When the light came around again, she was crying, without any sound.

"She turned seventeen," Nora said. "In April. And she was so— Priya, you know what she was like. She was so *alive*. And I wanted to give her something that meant something. Not a gift card. Something real. So I went to Kessler's and asked Darryl for the coat. And it was gone. They don't make it anymore." Her hands tightened in her lap. "So I gave her mine. In my car, in the school lot, on her birthday. I told her I'd had it forever and I wanted her to have it. And she put it on, and it was too small, and she didn't care. She wore it all summer."

"You didn't tell her about the other one," I said.

"No."

"Why?"

Nora was quiet for a long time.

"I've asked myself that," she said finally. "I told myself it didn't come up. That's not true. I think I didn't want to say Rowan's name to her. I didn't want Rowan anywhere near it." She looked down at her scarred hand. "And I think some part of me wanted to give her something that was mine. Because of what I was already doing to her. I think I wanted her to have a piece of me without knowing it, the way—" She stopped. "The way he did."

* * *

My dad's hands came out of his pockets.

I don't think Nora saw it. I saw it. He didn't do anything with them. He just stood there with his hands at his sides, open, and I watched his fingers curl and uncurl once.

"Theo," he said. His voice was very low.

"I know what you're thinking," Nora said.

"I don't think you do."

"I know what it was. I'm not going to sit here and pretend I don't know what it was." Her voice was steadier than it had any right to be. "My marriage has been over for years. Daniel and I live in the same house like two people in a hotel. And Theo's grandmother was dying, and I was over there every day, and he was so lonely, Cal, he was so lonely in that house, and he talked to me like I was a person, not furniture, not the lady who brings casseroles. And I let it become something it never should have been."

"He was sixteen," I said.

The words came out louder than I meant them to. They went out over the water and didn't come back.

Nora closed her eyes.

"He was sixteen," she said. "He was sixteen and I was forty-seven. I know. I have known it every single day. I told myself he was mature for his age. I told myself it was different. I told myself everything a person tells herself." She opened her eyes. "None of it's true. I knew it wasn't true while I was saying it."

I want to be clear about something, because people in this town have said a lot of things since then. People have called it an *affair*. People have said Theo was *involved with* her, like it was a club he joined. I've heard grown women at the grocery store say that he was *old enough to know better*.

He wasn't the one who was supposed to know better.

She was.

* * *

"Camille found out," Priya said.

"She saw him get out of my car. She went through his phone." Nora nodded slowly. "And she found the number. And somehow she'd already put it together with the coat. She'd been looking at me differently for weeks and I didn't know why. And then on that Monday night she texted me." Nora recited it from memory, in a voice like she was reading it off the inside of her own eyelids. "*I know who you are. I know about Theo. And I know about Rowan Ashby's jacket. Hollow Point. Midnight. Or I tell everyone tomorrow.*"

"So you came," I said.

"I came early. I sat in my car for twenty minutes. Then I got out and stood up there, at the top of the stairs, on the grass, and I waited." She looked over toward the stairs without moving her head. Just her eyes. "She texted me at 11:47. *I'm here.* And then she came up the path. In the coat. She'd worn it on purpose. She came right up to me and held her arms out and said, *Recognize this?*"

Priya made a small sound.

"I asked her to turn her location off." Nora's voice cracked for the first time. "I said I wasn't having this conversation with forty children watching a map. And she looked at me like I was disgusting, and she did it. She took out her phone and turned it off while she stared at me." Her breath shook. "That's what I think about most. Out of everything. That she did what I asked."

11:52.

I'd been looking at that number since the first morning.

* * *

"She said I'd done something to Rowan," Nora said. "She said I'd had a missing girl's jacket in my closet for six years and given it to her like a present. She said she'd been to the archive and seen the poster. She asked me what I did. She kept asking. *What did you do to her. What did you do.*"

"And you told her you bought two," I said.

"I told her I bought two. I told her about Kessler's. I told her she could ask Darryl." Nora shook her head slowly. "She didn't believe me. Why would she? I'd been lying to her for months. She said *you lie to everyone*. She said *you lie to my mom, you lie to Theo's mom, you lie to everybody, why would I believe you about anything.*" She swallowed. "She was right."

"Then what," Priya said.

"Then she said she was going to tell her mother. That night. And Theo's parents. And the police. And she turned to walk away. And I grabbed her arm."

The lighthouse beam came around. Went away.

"I just wanted her to stop walking away from me," Nora whispered. "That's all. I wanted five more minutes. I thought if I had five more minutes I could make her understand. So I grabbed her arm, and she yanked it away from me, hard. And she stumbled, and her foot came right out of her shoe. Her left shoe. She always wore them like slippers." Nora looked at Priya. "You know how she wore them."

Priya didn't answer. Her face was wet now. She didn't wipe it.

"She didn't even stop to get it. She just backed away from me. One shoe. Backward, toward the walkway, toward the railing, still looking at me like I was something she'd stepped in. And I said *Camille, stop, the railing, Camille*—and she said *don't you dare tell me what to do*—and she took one more step back, and the stone there is uneven, everybody knows it's uneven, and her heel caught on it." Nora's hands came up off her lap. Reaching for something in the air that wasn't there. "And she went back. Through the broken part. Where there's no rail at all."

Nobody moved.

"I grabbed for her," Nora said. "I got her foot. Her right foot. I had her by the ankle for one second. And the shoe came off in my hand."

She looked at her open hand. The pink scar across it.

"And I fell against the rail, and the shoe went out of my hand, and I heard it land on the rocks right below the edge, and I didn't hear her land at all." She was barely speaking now. "She didn't scream. That's the thing I hear. Every night. She didn't scream."

* * *

The ocean went in and out below us. The beam went around.

After a while my dad said, "And you didn't call anyone."

It wasn't a question.

"No," Nora said.

"You didn't call 911."

"No."

"She was down there," my dad said. His voice was shaking. "She was down there, and you got in your car."

"I called her name. Over and over. I couldn't see anything. It was so dark. I got down on my stomach on that path and called her name and nothing came back." Nora's face was wet. "And then I got up and I ran. I got in my car and I drove home and I sat in my garage with the engine off for two hours. And I told myself if she was alive, I'd call, I'd call right now. And I told myself if she was gone, calling wouldn't change anything. And I told myself both of those things at the same time, over and over, until it got light." She shut her eyes. "I didn't call, Cal. I never called."

My dad turned away. He walked a few steps out onto the gravel and stood there with his back to us, his shoulders shaking. I'd never seen him like that. Not even the night in Rowan's room.

I knew what he was thinking. I didn't need him to tell me. A girl went out into the dark, and nobody went looking for her until morning.

* * *

"The shoe," I said. My voice sounded strange to me. Very calm. "You came back for it."

Nora nodded. "Sitting in my garage, about four in the morning, I realized. It was on the ledge right by the broken railing. With my hands all over it. It would show somebody had been standing there. That she didn't just slip on the walkway. That somebody had been with her at the top." She looked at the stairs. "So I came back. In the dark. And I lay down on that path and reached through the broken rail and got it. And the rail cut my hand open. I didn't even feel it until I was in the car."

"You didn't see the other one," I said. "The left one. In the grass."

"I didn't even know it had come off. Not really. It was so dark, and the fog—" She shook her head. "I found out on Tuesday afternoon. Your dad told me. He said, *Wren found a shoe up there before the police did.*" She looked at my dad's back. "I almost dropped my coffee."

"And you asked if I'd seen her up close."

"Because of the coat." She looked at me. "You'd looked at that poster for six years. If you'd seen that lining, you'd have known it. I knew you would. I was so afraid you would. And when you said no, not really—" She pressed her scarred hand flat against her chest. "I believed you. I wanted to believe you so badly."

"I lied," I said. "I saw it on the stairs. I knew it from the first morning."

Nora closed her eyes.

* * *

The rest came out of her like something she'd been holding in her mouth too long.

The text to Theo, at 5:47 in the morning, sitting in her car in her own driveway with her hand wrapped in a dish towel. *You were at Colby's all night.* Telling him that he'd be blamed. That he was the last one on camera. Making him afraid so he'd stay quiet.

"I made a seventeen-year-old lie for me," she said. "I put that on him. On top of everything else I'd already put on him."

The casserole. Driving over to our house that afternoon to sit in our kitchen and find out what I'd seen.

Priya's aunt's kitchen on that first Sunday night, Nora standing at the sink with her hands in the dishwater, and Priya saying, from the other room, *Wren Ashby thinks Camille's jacket means something. She keeps asking where it came from.* And then Theo calling her, an hour later, on the T phone, scared out of his mind, saying Priya told him the same thing.

"I downloaded one of those apps," Nora said. "That Thursday. My niece showed me once how to use it, to send a joke to her friends. I sat in my car for an hour before I sent it. *Stop asking about the jacket.*" She looked at me. "I thought if I scared you, you'd stop. Like I'd scared him. I'm so sorry, honey."

"Don't," I said.

She flinched.

And the funeral. Calling my dad that Thursday to make sure we'd come. Leaving the reception with her keys. Saying something about ice.

"I did the Nora," she said, very quietly, and I understood that Rowan had told her about the name. "I lifted the door and leaned on it and it went up, the way it always used to. I went up to your room. I knew which one was yours. I painted that room, did you know that? Your dad and I painted it yellow the summer you turned five." She looked at her hands. "I found your box. I looked at everything. I needed to know what you had. If you had anything with me in it. If there was a picture of me in my coat." She shook her head. "There was just Rowan. Laughing. In hers. And the poster. And I sat down on your bed and I couldn't breathe for a while." She looked up. "I put it back wrong. Didn't I. I tried to put it back exactly."

"Stain to the wall," I said.

She didn't understand. That was all right. She didn't need to.

* * *

Headlights came up the road behind us. Two cars. Then the sound of tires on gravel, and doors, and boots.

Nora heard it. She straightened a little on the bench. She smoothed her coat over her knees, the way you'd smooth your skirt before church.

She looked at me one more time.

"There's something you should know," she said quickly. "About Rowan. I never told anyone, because it was nothing. I thought it was nothing." She glanced toward the lot, toward the flashlights. "That last week. Before the bonfire. She came by my house. She sat in my kitchen and drank a soda and didn't say anything for a long time. And then she asked me if I could keep a secret. If it wasn't hers."

I couldn't move.

"I said, *honey, I'm the best secret keeper in Quiet Hollow*." Nora's mouth twisted. "And she laughed and said *yeah, that's what I'm worried about.* And then she said never mind. She said it wasn't hers to tell, and she just needed to figure out if it was the kind of thing you could fix." Nora looked at me. "That's all. That's all I know. I always thought it was a boy. I thought it was nothing."

Flashlight beams swept across the gravel. Brennan's voice, sharp: "Nora Petrakis. Stay where you are. Hands where I can see them."

Nora put her hands on her knees, palms up. The pink line across her right one caught the light.

Reyes came out of the fog about ten steps behind Brennan. Her hair had come all the way out of its knot. She was breathing hard. She looked at Nora on the bench, and at my dad standing on the gravel with his back to all of us, and at Priya, and at me.

The look she gave me said several things. Most of them I'd hear later, in detail, at volume.

Then she turned to Nora, and her face went smooth and professional, all the way.

"Nora Petrakis," Reyes said. "Stand up for me, please."

Nora stood. She didn't resist. She held out her wrists before anybody asked her to, and Brennan put the cuffs on, and Reyes said the words. The right to remain silent. Anything you say. I'd heard them on television a thousand times. They sounded different in the fog at the edge of a cliff.

Priya hadn't said anything in a long time. As Brennan started to walk Nora toward the lot, she stepped forward. Into the flashlights.

Nora stopped. "Priya," she said. "Sweetheart. I held you the day you were born. I need you to know that was real. Whatever else wasn't—"

"Did she say anything?" Priya said. "At the end. Before. Did she say anything about her mom?"

Nora looked at her for a long moment.

"She said *my mom trusted you*," Nora said.

Priya's face didn't move. She stood there in the light and took it, all of it, and her voice when it came was steady in a way that scared me.

"Don't ever call me sweetheart again," she said.

Brennan walked Nora down the gravel path to the lot. We watched the flashlights go, smaller and smaller, until they were just two points of light in the fog, and then a car door, and then nothing.

The lighthouse beam went around. Eleven seconds. Then again.

* * *

My dad came back from the edge of the gravel. His face was wet and he didn't seem to care. He walked straight to me and put his arms around me, hard, the way he used to when I was little and had a nightmare, and he held on.

"You're here," he said into my hair. "You're here. You're right here."

My phone was buzzing in my pocket. It had been buzzing for a while. I didn't need to look to know.

Des. Watching my dot. Making sure it kept moving.

I texted him one-handed, over my dad's shoulder.

Reyes came over to us. She stood there a minute, looking at my dad and me, and at Priya, who was standing alone at the edge of the gravel with her arms wrapped around herself, looking at the place where her cousin had gone over.

"I told you to stay home," Reyes said.

"I know."

"I told you she might—"

"I know."

She looked at my dad. "And you."

"I know," my dad said.

Reyes was quiet. Then she sighed, long, and rubbed her face with both hands, and when she took them away she just looked tired. The real tired, the bottom layer, the one she usually kept hidden.

"Go home," she said. "All three of you. I'll come by tomorrow and take everybody's statements properly. Tonight, go home."

My dad let go of me. He went over to Priya and stood next to her for a moment. Then he took off his coat and put it around her shoulders. It was too big. It hung off her like a cape.

She looked down at it. Then up at him.

"Come on," my dad said gently. "Let's get you home to your mom."

We walked back down the gravel path to the truck, the three of us. Behind us the beam swept out over the water and came back again, marking exactly where the rocks start, the way it always has.

I didn't look back at the railing.

I'd seen it.

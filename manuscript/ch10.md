# Chapter Ten

My dad was in the kitchen in his undershirt and his funeral pants, filling the kettle. His hair was still wet. He looked older than he had that morning. Funerals do that to everyone, I think, but they do it to him more.

"You want tea?" he said, without turning around.

"Dad. I need to tell you something."

He heard it in my voice. I watched him hear it. He turned off the faucet and set the kettle down on the counter, very carefully, like it might go off.

"Okay," he said.

I'd been trying to figure out how to start for the whole length of the stairs. In the end I just started at the beginning, because there isn't any other place to start a thing like that.

I told him about the jacket on the cliff stairs. The lining. The smell. I told him about Rowan's last post and the poster in my shoebox and the line that said *last seen wearing*. I told him about the Fuel Stop camera and the 4:58 car and Camille in the archive asking if they ever found the jacket. I told him about the text on Thursday night. And then I told him about the garage door, and the shoebox, and the photo of Rowan lying face down.

He didn't say anything the whole time. He stood at the counter with his hand flat on it, the way he does, like he was checking whether it was still solid.

When I got to the shoebox, he took his hand off the counter.

"Somebody was in this house," he said.

"Yes."

"Today. While we were at that church."

"Yes."

"In your room."

"Yes."

He walked out of the kitchen. I heard him go up the stairs, fast, two at a time, which I haven't heard him do in years. I heard my closet door. Then nothing for a long time.

When I went up, he was standing in the middle of my room looking at the top shelf of my closet. He didn't touch it. He just looked.

"Reyes is on her way," I said quietly.

"The detective."

"She said I had to tell you before she got here."

He turned around and looked at me. And I'd been braced for a lot of things. Yelling. The jaw thing. The closed laptop. The *I asked you to let this go*.

What I hadn't been braced for was that he'd look scared.

My dad doesn't look scared. My dad looked tired for six years, and sad, and careful, and sometimes angry in the quiet way that's worse than loud. But he never looked scared. Not in front of me. I think he'd decided, when it happened, that one of us had to not look scared, and he'd picked himself.

He looked scared now.

"How long," he said.

"Since the morning she died."

"Two weeks." He sat down on the edge of my bed like his legs had made the decision without him. "Two weeks you've been doing this. And I've been sitting at that table every night."

"I know."

"You looked me in the eye and said you weren't looking for anything."

"I know."

"Wren." He put his face in his hands. When he spoke again, it came out from behind them, muffled. "I can't. I told you I can't do it twice. I meant it. I wasn't being dramatic."

"I'm not Rowan."

He took his hands away from his face and looked at me with red eyes. "You think I don't know that? You think I don't know the difference between my daughters?" His voice cracked straight down the middle. "She went out the door one night and she didn't come back, and I didn't stop her. I didn't even ask where she was going. I was so tired from the double shifts, I was asleep on the couch, and she said *bye Dad* and I said *be careful* without even opening my eyes. That's the last thing. *Be careful.* With my eyes shut."

I'd never heard that before. Not once in six years. I stood in the doorway of my room and didn't move.

"And now somebody's been in your room," he said. "Somebody went through her things, in your room, and I was at a funeral holding a paper plate."

"Dad."

"I'm not mad at you." He laughed, and it wasn't a laugh. "I'm so mad at you. I'm not mad at you. I don't know what I am."

I sat down next to him on the bed. Not touching. Just next to him. The way Des sits next to me.

After a while, he said, "That jacket."

"What about it?"

"She came home in it on the Fourth of July. That summer. It was too warm for it, and she had it on anyway, over a tank top, like a cape. I asked where she got it and she said somebody gave it to her at the bonfire. Said she'd been freezing and somebody gave it to her." He was looking at the floor. "I didn't ask who. I was glad somebody'd been kind to her. I didn't ask a lot of things that summer."

"Somebody gave it to her," I said. "At the Fourth of July bonfire."

"That's what she said." He looked at me. "Why? Does that matter?"

"I don't know yet," I said.

The doorbell rang.

* * *

Reyes was on the porch in her gray rain jacket with her hands in her pockets. She looked at my dad, and my dad looked at her, and something passed between them that I didn't have the words for. Two adults sizing each other up over a kid.

"Mr. Ashby," she said. "I'm sorry to meet you like this."

"You're the one who's been talking to my daughter."

"I'm the one who's been telling your daughter to go home," Reyes said. "She doesn't listen very well."

Something in my dad's face moved, almost. "No," he said. "She doesn't."

He let her in.

She looked at my closet for six minutes. She took photos of the shelf and the shoebox with her own phone, not a department camera. She asked me to show her exactly how I'd found it, and exactly how I'd left it the Saturday before, and she wrote it down in a small notebook. She checked the latch on my bedroom window. She checked the front door, the back door, the windows on the first floor.

Then she went into the garage and stood in front of the door and looked at it for a while.

"Show me," she said.

I showed her. Lift a foot. Lean left. Clunk. Shove.

She tried it. It took her three tries. On the third, it went up with its long complaining squeal and the gray afternoon light came pouring in under it.

"Anybody who's seen it done once could do it," she said. She let it fall shut and turned to my dad. "Who else has watched somebody open this?"

My dad rubbed his jaw. "Des. His mom, probably. Half the neighborhood. The mailman. That door's been broken since—" He stopped. "Since the girls were small."

"Your older daughter knew it."

"Rowan knew it. Everybody who used to come and go knew it."

Reyes wrote that down too. Then she looked at the door that goes from the garage into the kitchen, the one with no lock, and then at my dad.

"I'd put a deadbolt on that tonight," she said.

"I'll go to Kessler's."

"Good." She put the notebook in her pocket. "Mr. Ashby, I'm going to be honest with you, because I think you'd rather have that. I can't open a case on this. Nothing was taken. Nothing was damaged. There's no sign of forced entry. I write this up, it goes in a drawer, and the man who owns the drawer has already told me the thing your daughter's looking into is closed." She paused. "So I'm not writing it up. I'm keeping my own notes. And I'm telling you that I think somebody wanted to know what your daughter has, and came in here to find out."

"And what if they come back?"

"Then you'll have a deadbolt." Reyes looked at me. "And she'll have stopped giving them reasons to."

My dad looked at me too.

They were both looking at me. I've never felt more like a kid in my whole life.

"Who," my dad said. "Who would do this?"

Reyes was quiet for a second. I knew she wasn't going to say Theo's name. She was too careful. She wasn't going to give my dad a name to go drive over to with his funeral tie still in his pocket.

"Somebody who knows this house," she said. "And somebody who knew you'd be gone this afternoon. That's what I've got. That's all I've got."

She left at five. She stopped on the porch, and turned, and said to my dad, low, so I almost didn't hear it:

"She's good at this, you know. That's the problem. If she were bad at it, nobody would care what she has."

My dad didn't answer. He closed the door.

* * *

He went to Kessler's and came back with a deadbolt in a plastic package and spent the whole evening installing it. He swore at the drill. He swore at the strike plate. He measured three times and drilled once and the hole was still a little off. He didn't let me help.

At nine-thirty he was done. He turned the bolt back and forth. It made a heavy, solid sound.

"There," he said.

I was sitting on the garage couch with my knees up. He looked at me across all the junk, the whiteboard turned to face the wall, the workbench, the old bikes.

"I'm not going to tell you to stop," he said. "I've been telling you to stop for six years. I can see where that's gotten me."

"Dad—"

"Let me finish." He put the drill down on the workbench. "I'm going to tell you that if you get a text like that again, I hear about it that night. Not two weeks later. That night. Before the detective. Before Des. Me."

"Okay."

"I don't want okay. I want you to say it like you mean it longer than six seconds."

I looked at him. He'd known. All this time, he'd known about the six seconds.

"I'll tell you," I said. "That night."

He nodded. He picked up the empty deadbolt package and turned it over in his hands. Then he turned to the whiteboard, the one facing the wall, the one he'd bought for fantasy football and used once, and he looked at it for a long time.

He didn't turn it around.

"Goodnight, Wren," he said.

* * *

I told Des everything on Sunday morning, sitting on his back porch with two mugs of the terrible instant coffee his mom keeps for guests who don't know better. The text. The shoebox. Reyes testing the garage door. My dad on the edge of my bed, saying *be careful* with his eyes shut.

Des didn't say much while I talked. From Des, that's its own kind of alarm bell. When I was done, he put his mug down on the railing and looked out at his yard for a while like he was recalculating something.

"Okay," he said. "That settles it."

"Settles what?"

"We need Theo's alibi broken, and we need it broken now. Because whoever's going in your house isn't going to wait for us to be careful."

"I didn't tell you so you'd do something reckless."

"I know you didn't. I'm doing it anyway." There was an edge in his voice I don't hear often. Past his usual anxious-funny. Closer to actually angry. "Somebody went through your dead sister's things while you were at a funeral, Wren. I don't care about being careful anymore. I care about this being over."

I should have talked him out of it. I've thought about that a lot since. The version of that Sunday where I said no, let's slow down, and none of what came next happens.

But I didn't say that. Because some ugly, honest part of me needed him to be angry for me exactly as much as he was. So I let him be.

* * *

Here's what we knew, and what I didn't fully understand yet.

Colby Reed's server had a pinned message at the top of the photos channel. It was a link to a shared album. That's how a lot of friend groups do it. Discord shrinks your photos and strips half the information out, so people just dump the real full-size ones into a shared album and post the link. Everyone in the server can see it. Everyone in the server can add to it.

And a shared album shows you things the screenshots don't. You tap a photo, you hit the little *info* button, and it tells you when the photo was actually taken. Not when it was posted. Not what the caption says. When the camera clicked.

Des wasn't in Colby's server. Neither of us was. We've never run in Theo's circle and we'd never needed to.

But a private server only stays private as long as nobody inside decides to let someone in. And Des spent all of Sunday afternoon working an angle on a sophomore named Eli Brandt. Eli is on JV swim and was at Colby's on Monday night, because his older brother is on the soccer team and dragged him along. Des knew him a little from robotics club freshman year. After two days of polite, mostly harmless pestering, Eli added an account Des had made under a name that wasn't his. No questions asked. The way fifteen-year-olds let people into things all the time without thinking twice about who's asking.

He texted me Tuesday during lunch. Three words.

[[LOG]]
Des: found something. maybe.
[[/LOG]]

* * *

He was in the AV room. He runs the streaming club, so he has a key, which the school has never once thought through. He had three tabs open and a look on his face I've seen maybe twice. The specific focus he gets when a puzzle is actually giving something back.

"Okay," he said, without saying hi. "So. Remember the two photos from Colby's captions? Theo at the air hockey table at 11:15. Theo on the couch at 11:52."

"Yes."

"They're in the shared album. The originals. Look."

He tapped one. Theo bent over the air hockey table, grinning. He hit *info*.

I stared at the screen.

"Not 11:15," Des said. "12:21. Not 11:52. 12:38." He said it carefully, like he didn't trust the words yet either. "Like an hour off, both of them. Same direction."

"Could the phone's clock be wrong?"

"That's what I thought. That happens. Phones get weird." He scrolled. "But there's like sixty other photos in this album from Monday night, from like fifteen different people's phones. I checked a bunch of them. The times all line up with each other. If Colby's phone was wrong, his would be off from everybody else's. And it's not. His other photos match everybody's." He sat back. "His clock's fine. His captions aren't."

"So the photos are real. He just lied about when he took them."

"He took two photos of Theo after Theo came back. And then on Tuesday afternoon, after everybody found out, he posted them with fake times on them so everybody would screenshot them and pass them around. And they did." Des shook his head slowly. "He didn't have to fake anything. He just had to write a caption. Nobody checks a caption."

"There aren't any photos of Theo from before midnight?"

"There's one at 10:47, him on the couch, and then nothing with him in it until 12:21. That's an hour and a half gap." Des looked at me. "11:40's right in the middle of it."

I wanted to hug him. I didn't, because we don't really do that. Eight years of friendship built on never being sincere about anything for more than four seconds at a time. But I wanted to.

"Screenshot those," I said.

"I did. But screenshots are screenshots. If I'm handing this to Reyes, I want the actual files. Downloaded. The original things, with all the data still in them. Otherwise it's my word and a picture of a screen."

"How long does that take?"

"Not long. I'll do it tonight. It's a lot of photos and I want all of them, not just two, so nobody can say I cherry-picked." He closed the laptop. "Tonight. Then we've got it."

* * *

That's when it went wrong.

I only got the whole story later, in pieces. Des doesn't like telling stories about himself where he doesn't come out looking at least a little competent. He told me this one looking at his own hands the whole time.

Colby runs that server. He keeps half an eye on it. And on Tuesday night, around ten, Colby noticed an account he didn't recognize downloading every single photo from Monday night out of the shared album. One after another, fast, all of them. The kind of pattern that doesn't look like a bored kid scrolling. It looks exactly like what it was.

Colby removed the account from the album before the download finished. Then he looked up who'd added it to the server. Then he got Eli Brandt on a call.

Eli gave up Des's name almost immediately. Des was very clear about this part. He insisted on it. Not because Eli was mean. Because he was fifteen and getting yelled at by a call full of seniors, and he did what you do when you're fifteen and scared, which is hand over whoever got you into it as fast as you can.

They found Des in the student lot after school on Wednesday.

Three of them. Colby and two soccer guys. They didn't touch him. They didn't do anything anyone could have pointed to later and called an incident. They just stood close enough, around his car, and said enough. Des described it afterward as the scariest completely nonviolent conversation of his entire life.

"They knew everything," he told me. We were sitting in his car in my driveway. He hadn't turned the engine off. His voice was flat in a way I didn't like. "They knew I was the account. They knew what I was downloading. Colby said, and I'm quoting, because I've replayed this about forty times, *You should think real hard about whose business you're in. Some of us have older brothers who don't think as carefully as we do.*"

"That's a threat."

"It's a threat shaped like a compliment about my thinking skills, yeah." He tried to smile. It didn't land. From Des, that told me more than anything. "I laughed it off. I said something dumb, I don't even remember what. Then I got in the car and sat in the lot for twenty minutes before I could drive, because my hands wouldn't stop shaking."

He held them up. They were steady now. He looked at them like they belonged to somebody else.

"I've never been scared like that, Wren," he said. "Not this whole time. Not the camera, not the car at five AM. That stuff felt like a puzzle. This felt like a door. Like I'd opened a door and there was a room behind it with people in it."

I didn't know what to say. That happens to me almost as rarely as it happens to him. So for once we just sat there in the car not saying anything, looking out the windshield at my garage door.

"I need to step back," he said finally. "Not forever. I'm not bailing on you. I need you to hear that part specifically, because I know how it's going to sound. But I can't be the guy pulling files off a server with three guys in it who apparently have older brothers. Not right now."

"Okay."

"That's it? Okay?"

"You just told me you were scared in a way you've never been scared before, and you're asking me if it's okay to be careful. I'm not going to make you argue me into that." It cost me something to say it that easily. I think he could tell. But I meant it. "I got you into this. I don't get to be mad that you found the edge."

"I hate that you're being reasonable. It's very unlike you."

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain like four moods, and reasonable isn't usually one of them." But he almost laughed. Actually laughed, a little, the first real one since he'd started talking. Something in my chest let go a notch.

"I'm not gone," he said. "Research. Stuff that doesn't put me in a parking lot with anybody's brother. I'm just not doing *that* again."

"That's more than fair."

He drove home. I stood in the driveway and watched his taillights go, both of them equally bright, and thought about the screenshots on his phone. Two photos, an hour off. The proof that Theo Marsh's alibi was a caption and nothing else.

Screenshots. Not files. Not the real thing. Not something that would survive Reyes raising one eyebrow at it.

And the only people who still had the originals now knew exactly who'd been looking.

There had to be another way into that album.

It was going to have to be mine.

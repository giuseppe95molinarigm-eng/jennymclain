# Chapter Sixteen

Reyes got to the school in eleven minutes. She came in through the library doors in her gray rain jacket, and Ms. Feiler stood up from the front desk without a word and walked her to the archive and opened the door.

I didn't go in. Reyes looked at me once, at the end of the stacks, and I understood. This part wasn't mine.

Priya came out after a while. She sat down next to me on the floor between the biographies and the true crime. Her eyes were red. She'd stopped crying.

"His mom's coming," she said. "Reyes called her. And she's calling a different lawyer. A real one. One who works for him." She pressed her palms into her eyes. "Not one who works for his dad's cousin."

"Is he okay?"

"No." She dropped her hands. "But I think he's better than he was yesterday. I think yesterday he was the only person in the world who knew, and now he isn't." She stared at the shelf across from us. A whole row of books with women's faces on the covers and red titles. *She Vanished. The Woman Next Door.* "He kept asking me if Cam hated him. At the end. If she died hating him."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I didn't know." Her voice was very small. "Because I don't. And I'm not going to lie to him. Everybody's been lying to him for a year."

We sat there until Theo's mom came running through the library doors with her car keys still in her hand. She didn't look at us. She went straight to the archive, and through the crack in the door I saw her drop to her knees on the floor next to her son.

* * *

My dad was in the kitchen when I got home. He looked at my face and turned off the stove.

I told him. All of it. The receipt. Darryl and April. The headlight on Larch Street. The suspect board at four in the morning. The archive, and Theo on the floor, and the screenshot that said 5:47.

I told him who T was.

He didn't say anything for a long time. He stood at the counter with his hand flat on it. Then he took his hand off the counter and looked at it, like he'd forgotten it belonged to him.

"She sat right there," he said. He pointed at the chair across from him. The one by the window. "On the Tuesday. She sat in that chair with her coat on and drank a cup of coffee I made her, and she asked me about you." His voice was very quiet. "She asked if you'd seen anything. She asked if you were okay. I said you were tough. I said, *Wren's fine, she's always fine.*" He looked at me. "I thought she was being kind."

"I know."

"And on Thursday she called. Asked if we were coming to the funeral. Said Amrita would appreciate it." He swallowed. "So I made sure we went. I put on my suit and I made sure we were both gone from this house all afternoon."

"Dad. You couldn't have known."

"I let her in," he said. "That's what I keep— I *let* her in. Years ago I gave her a key. For Saturdays. She had a key to this house for years. When she stopped coming, I never asked for it back. I never even thought about it. And the garage—" He stopped.

"What about the garage?"

He rubbed his face. "She's the one who figured it out. The trick. When we first moved in. Rowan kept forgetting her key, and Nora stood out there one Saturday with a bag of groceries and fiddled with it till it went up, and then she showed Rowan. Lift, lean, shove." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "Rowan called it *the Nora*. Do the Nora, she'd say. For years. I'd forgotten that. I'd completely forgotten that."

I stood very still in the kitchen.

*Do the Nora.*

Des learned it in fourth grade. Before that, it was me, and Dad, and Rowan, and whoever was watching us on Saturdays.

Whoever was watching us had invented it.

* * *

Here's what happened over the next two days, the way Reyes explained it to me later, and the way I found out the rest on my own.

Theo gave his statement Monday night. In the Marshes' living room, with his mom on one side of him and the new lawyer on the other, and his dad standing in the kitchen doorway looking like somebody had taken a sledgehammer to the floor under him. Reyes recorded it. It took three hours.

Kenzie Ochoa gave hers on Tuesday morning, before school, with her mom. Reyes said Kenzie brought her retainer case to the interview and held it the entire time, like a stress ball.

Colby Reed gave his Tuesday afternoon. With his parents and a lawyer. Reyes said he cried. She said he kept saying *I thought I was helping him, I thought I was helping my friend*, and she believed him, and it didn't matter.

Delgado drove his little drive from the desk drawer down to the department himself. Reyes said he made her sign for it in front of him. Twice.

Darryl opened his safe.

And on Monday night, at 9:40, while Theo was still talking, Reyes called the county district attorney at home.

Sheriff Lockhart found out Tuesday morning. Reyes didn't tell me much about that conversation. She said it happened in his office with the door closed. She said it was loud, on his end. She said that at one point he told her she was finished in Quiet Hollow, and that she'd answered that a seventeen-year-old had just reported that an adult woman sexually abused him, and that a sheriff who sat on that report for any reason at all would be finished somewhere a lot bigger than Quiet Hollow.

"He didn't have a lot to say after that," Reyes said.

"Were you scared?"

She thought about it. "Yes," she said. "Ask me again in a year if it was worth it."

* * *

The DA's office moved faster than I knew offices could move. Reyes said that's what happens when a case has a minor in it. Everybody's desk clears.

By Tuesday night there was a judge's signature on four separate pieces of paper.

A search warrant for 62 Larch Street.

A search warrant for a dark green wagon registered to Nora and Daniel Petrakis.

A warrant for Nora's cell phone, and for the carrier records of a prepaid number ending in 4417.

And a call to a lab in Portland from somebody much more important than Reyes, asking why there was a swab from Hollow Point sitting in a queue marked non-priority, and saying that as of right now it was the only priority they had.

"Wednesday morning," Reyes told me. "Early. And you're not coming, and you're not driving past, and you're not sitting in Des's car at the end of Larch Street with binoculars."

"I wasn't going to—"

"Wren."

"I was thinking about it," I admitted.

"I know you were," she said. "Go to school. I'll call you."

* * *

I didn't go to school.

My dad didn't either. He called the mill at six-thirty Wednesday morning and said he had a family thing. Then he called the school and said I was sick. He hung up and looked at me across the kitchen, where I was sitting with my backpack already on.

"You're not going," he said.

"Dad—"

"You're not going to sit in chemistry while this happens. And I'm not going to sit at the mill." He took my backpack off my shoulders, gently, and set it on the floor. "We're going to sit here. Together. And wait for that phone to ring."

So we did.

He made pancakes. Neither of us ate them. He did the crossword in the paper with a pen, which he only does when he's nervous, because he says if you're going to be wrong you should be wrong in ink. I sat at the table with my phone face up in front of me and watched the screen not light up.

Des texted every fifteen minutes from first period, then second.

[[LOG]]
Des: anything
Des: anything??
Des: I'm in AP gov and Mr. Haskins is talking about the electoral college and I'm going to scream
Des: priya says she can't feel her hands
Des: anything
[[/LOG]]

At 8:15, Reyes texted one word.

[[LOG]]
Reyes: In.
[[/LOG]]

Then nothing. For three hours.

My dad finished the crossword. He started the second crossword, the hard one in the back section, which he never finishes. I cleaned my whole room, which I've never done voluntarily in my life, and then I sat on my floor and looked at the top shelf of my closet. The shoebox was back there now, stain to the wall. I'd fixed it. I didn't take it down.

At 11:22 my phone rang.

* * *

"Okay," Reyes said. She sounded strange. Wrung out. "I'm going to tell you some things, and then I'm going to tell you a thing you're not going to like."

"Okay."

"The closet in the main bedroom. Back left corner." I heard her breathe. "Cedar. Everywhere. Those little blocks you hang on the rod. Cedar rings on the hangers. A basket of loose blocks on the floor. The whole closet smells like the inside of a hope chest. You open the door and it hits you." A pause. "Hanging on the left side, there's a garment bag with a Kessler's Supply tag still zip-tied to the hanger. It's empty. Size small."

I closed my eyes.

"On the top shelf," Reyes said, "behind a stack of folded sweaters, there's a shoebox. Inside the shoebox there's a plastic Kessler's bag, folded over twice. And inside the bag there's a white sneaker. Women's eight and a half. Little stitched logo on the heel." Her voice was very even. "Right foot. The heel's crushed flat. Walked down under. There's a rust-colored stain on the toe, and some on the laces, and the laces are still tied."

I didn't say anything. I couldn't.

At 6:52 on a Tuesday morning in October, I'd crouched in wet grass with my phone flashlight pointed at a shoe. One shoe. The left one. And I'd stood on a cliff landing and told a detective it was a left, and asked her where the right one was.

*I still had a shoe to find.*

It had been on a shelf on Larch Street the whole time. Behind the sweaters. In a house with a wreath on the door. Three and a half weeks, while she ran Camille's mother's kitchen and hugged me at the vigil and drove past my house to see if my dad's truck was in the driveway.

"Wren?"

"I'm here."

"There's more," Reyes said. "In her dresser, top drawer, there's a box of cards. Every birthday card, every thank-you note, every Christmas card she's gotten in twenty-five years, I think. She keeps everything. There's one from your sister." A pause. "It's from that July. Rowan drew a little jacket on the front. It says *thanks for the hand-me-down!! never giving it back.*"

I put my hand over my mouth. My dad, across the table, put his pen down.

"And Theo's in there too," Reyes said, quieter. "Notes. Things he gave her. I'm not going to tell you about those. They're his."

"Okay," I said, behind my hand.

"Now the part you're not going to like."

I waited.

"She's not here," Reyes said. "She called in sick to the dental office at seven-thirty this morning. Her husband says she left the house before he woke up. Her car's gone. Her phone's off. Not dead, off. The last place it pinged was the tower out by the harbor, at 6:50 this morning." She breathed out. "Theo hasn't answered her since Monday. She's been texting him on the T number. *Are you okay. Why aren't you answering. Please just tell me you're okay.* The last one was at 5:15 this morning. *I'm sorry. I'm so sorry for all of it.*"

My dad was standing up now. He'd come around the table. He was standing next to my chair.

"She knows," I said.

"She knows something's happening. She doesn't know what." Reyes's voice changed. "There's an alert out for the car. Every deputy in the county has it, and the state police. Brennan's in Astoria checking her sister's. She's not going to get far in a green wagon with one headlight." A pause. "But, Wren. If she contacts you. If she calls, if she texts, if she shows up at your door. You don't answer. You call me. You understand?"

"I understand."

"I'll call you when we have her."

* * *

She didn't call.

Noon. One o'clock. Two. Des and Priya came over after school and the four of us sat in the kitchen, my dad and Des and Priya and me, and nobody talked very much. Priya had a phone in each hand, hers and the cracked old one she uses for the Beacon, refreshing the county's alerts page and the police scanner app over and over. Des had his laptop open to nothing.

At three-thirty, Priya's mom called her. Priya listened, said *okay* four times, and hung up.

"My aunt knows," she said. "Reyes went to tell her in person." She stared at the table. "My mom says she's just sitting on the couch. She keeps asking for Nora. She keeps forgetting and asking where Nora is."

Nobody said anything to that. What could you say.

At four, Delgado called me.

I hadn't even known Delgado had my number. I found out later he'd gotten it from Kessler's, because Darryl had it from the time I found his reading glasses in the paint aisle.

"Your detective's not picking up," he said, without hello. "Busy, I guess. You tell her something for me."

"Okay."

"That green car. The one with the eye." He cleared his throat. "Since you two came in, I've been watching the road. Can't help it now. Ever since that night, that car's gone out to the point four, five times. Always after dark. Always comes back in about an hour. Last Thursday. Sunday before that." He paused. "It went out again about twenty minutes ago. Hasn't come back."

I was standing up. I didn't remember standing up.

"Twenty minutes ago," I said.

"I'm looking at the road right now," Delgado said. "It hasn't come back."

* * *

I called Reyes. She answered on the second ring, and I could hear wind and an engine and somebody else's radio.

"She's at the point," I said. "Delgado's been watching. She's been going out there after dark for three weeks. She went out twenty minutes ago."

Silence. Just the wind.

"Okay," Reyes said. "Okay. Brennan's closest, she's coming back from Astoria, she's twenty-five minutes out. I'm on the far side of the county, I'm forty." I heard her say something to someone else, sharp. Then back to me. "Wren. Listen to me. You stay home. Do you hear me? You stay in that house."

"She's not going to hurt anybody."

"You don't know that. You don't know what a person does when it's all coming down. Nobody does." Her voice was steel. "Stay home."

She hung up.

I stood in the kitchen with my phone in my hand. Des and Priya were both on their feet. Priya's face had gone completely white.

"She goes out there," Priya said. "She's been going out there. To where Cam—" She couldn't finish it.

"People go back," I said. "That's what she does. She went back the first night. She keeps going back."

I thought about the railing. The rust. The broken place. A woman with nowhere left to go, in the dark, standing at the edge of the exact spot.

I thought about Reyes, twenty-five minutes away. Forty minutes away.

I looked at my dad.

He was already looking at me. He'd heard every word. He was standing by the hook where his keys hang, and I watched his face, and I watched something happen in it that I don't think I'd ever seen before. Six years of fear, and six years of keeping his head above water, and six years of *be careful* with his eyes shut.

He took his keys off the hook.

"Dad," I said. "Reyes said stay home."

"Reyes doesn't know Nora," my dad said. "I do." He put on his coat. "Get in the truck."

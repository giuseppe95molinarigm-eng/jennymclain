# Chapter Eight

I asked Theo Marsh about the jacket on Tuesday, between second and third period. I'd like to say it was a strategic decision. It wasn't. He was at his locker, and I was walking past it, and the question was out of my mouth before the part of my brain that makes plans could weigh in.

He was alone, which almost never happens. Theo usually travels in a pack. That week the pack had thinned out. People were being careful around him in a way that looked like kindness from far away and like something else up close.

He was leaning his weight on his good leg, digging for a book, his right knee strapped into a black brace with metal hinges on the sides. He looked bad. Not sad bad. Tired bad, like he hadn't slept since the weekend before last. There was a gray tinge under his tan.

"Hey," I said. "Theo."

He looked up. It took him a second to place me, which is fair. We've had maybe two conversations in our lives, both about a group project.

"Wren," he said. Then something in his face shut, like a window somebody had remembered to close. "If this is about the stupid photo with the caption, I didn't post that."

"It's not about the photo."

"Then I'm kind of not—"

"The jacket Camille was wearing," I said. "The navy one. Do you know where she got it?"

I've watched a lot of people get asked a lot of questions. Lost-retainer questions. Where-were-you-when-the-bike-disappeared questions. Most people's faces do one of two things. They go blank because they don't know, or they light up because they do.

Theo's did neither.

The color went out of it. All of it, all at once, like somebody had pulled a plug. His hand stopped moving inside the locker. For one long second, he just looked at me.

Then he looked down at his phone. Not like he'd gotten a text. Like he was checking whether it was still there.

"Why are you asking me that?" he said.

"Because I'm asking."

"It's just a jacket." His voice had gone strange and flat. "She just had it. She had it all summer. I don't know. Ask somebody else."

"Ask who?"

He slammed the locker. Not hard. Just hard enough. "Ask somebody who wants to talk about it," he said. "I don't. She's dead, Wren. You get that, right? Everybody keeps acting like this is some kind of—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Leave it alone."

He walked away, fast, the brace making his right leg swing out stiff on every step. Exactly like the figure on the camera, walking back into the dark.

I stood there until the bell rang.

* * *

On Wednesday, Sheriff Boone Lockhart held a press conference on the courthouse steps.

The medical examiner's report had come back. Everybody knew because somebody's aunt works at the county office and texted somebody's cousin, which is how the news has always traveled in Quiet Hollow, faster than the actual news.

Half the school watched it live in the cafeteria, crowded around phones propped against napkin dispensers. People cluster around bad news the way they cluster around a bonfire. Close enough to feel it. Not so close it burns.

Sheriff Lockhart has a face made for local news. I don't mean that as a compliment. I mean he has the kind of face that reads as trustworthy on a small screen at six o'clock. Square jaw. Easy voice. Silver at the temples in a way that looks deliberate. He's been sheriff since before I was born, and he has the practiced stillness of a man who's stood in front of a camera enough times to forget it's there.

Deputy Brennan stood two steps behind him and to the left, hands clasped in front of her, looking at the middle distance. I didn't see Reyes anywhere.

"The medical examiner has completed her review," Lockhart said. "Her findings are consistent with an accidental fall from the Hollow Point walkway. There is no evidence of foul play. The department considers this matter closed." He paused for exactly the right amount of time. "Our hearts go out to the Malhotra family. We ask that the community respect their privacy, and we remind everyone that the walkway is closed from sunset to sunrise for a reason."

A reporter from the Astoria station asked about the railing. Lockhart said the county was "actively reviewing" repairs. He's said that at every town meeting for ten years.

Then somebody I couldn't see, a woman's voice off camera, asked, "Sheriff, given the location, has there been any consideration of a connection to the Rowan Ashby case?"

Every head at my lunch table turned to look at me. I kept my eyes on the screen.

Lockhart didn't blink. Something in his face went smooth instead. Not blank. Smooth, like water closing over a stone.

"None whatsoever," he said. "Those are two very different tragedies, and I'd ask everyone to be careful about making that kind of association. It's not fair to either family." He nodded at the cameras. "Thank you."

Forty-one seconds. I timed it, because apparently I time things now.

Des watched it over my shoulder, chewing the end of a pen he wasn't writing with. "That's it? That's the whole thing?"

"That's the whole thing."

"No limp guy. No five AM car. Nothing about the jacket."

"Why would there be? We're not a source. We're two kids with a whiteboard and a gas station USB drive."

"Delgado gave us that USB legally."

"Delgado gave it to us because I know where his generator was. That's not chain of custody, Des."

He didn't argue. He knew. He just didn't like hearing it out loud.

The cafeteria went quiet for about one beat, and then the volume came right back up, like nothing had happened. For most of those people nothing had. Not the kind of nothing that changes how you sleep. Camille got a hashtag and a vigil and forty-one seconds on the courthouse steps, and by Friday she'd be a name people said in the careful voice they use for anyone who died young in a small town. Sad. Far away. Filed.

I wasn't ready to file her.

Across the cafeteria, at the table by the windows, Theo was sitting with Colby and two of the soccer guys. They weren't watching the press conference. Colby was talking low, leaning in. Theo was staring at his tray. As I watched, Colby put a hand on the back of Theo's neck and gave it a little shake, the way guys do. *You're good. You're good.*

Theo didn't look good.

* * *

My dad was almost cheerful that night. In the quiet way he gets cheerful, which is mostly the line between his eyebrows going away for a few hours.

"Saw the news," he said. He was cutting onions at the counter, not looking up. That's how my dad brings things up. Sideways, with a knife in his hand. "Sounds like they got their answer."

"Sounds like they stopped looking for one."

He set the knife down. "Wren."

"I'm not doing anything. I'm allowed to have an opinion about a press conference."

"It's not the opinion I'm worried about." He picked the knife back up, which in my dad's language is the end of a conversation whether or not I'm finished with it. Then he put it down again, which almost never happens. He leaned on the counter with both hands.

"Somebody asked about your sister," he said. "At the end."

"I saw."

"I don't know who that was." He was looking at the onions. "Some reporter from out of town. They do that, every so often. Somebody new finds the story and thinks they're the first one to notice it." He breathed out. "Every time, for a week, people look at me in the grocery store again."

I didn't know what to say to that. My dad never talks about the grocery store. My dad never talks about any of it.

"I just want one season," he said, quiet, "where the answer is just the answer. That's all. One."

He went back to cutting onions. I went upstairs. Neither of us said anything else, because there wasn't a version of the truth I could hand him that wouldn't cost us both something. I'd already decided I wasn't ready to pay it yet.

But I sat on my bed afterward thinking about it. Some reporter from out of town. They do that, every so often.

He'd said it like a man describing the weather. Like something that happens, that you wait out.

I'd never thought before about what it must be like, being the father in a story strangers keep finding.

* * *

I found Reyes on Thursday.

Not exactly on purpose. I'd started taking the long way home after school, the way that goes past the sheriff's department. I told myself it was just a route. I used to tell myself checking Rowan's old account was just curiosity, too.

She was in the side lot. Off duty, by the look of her. Jeans and a gray rain jacket instead of the blazer. She was sitting on the open tailgate of her truck with a coffee next to her, staring at nothing, the way people stare when they're actually staring at something they can't say out loud. Her dark hair was coming loose from its knot. She looked like she'd been tired for about a decade and had recently stopped fighting it.

"You're not supposed to be here," she said, without turning her head.

It was the exact same sentence she'd said to me at the bottom of the cliff stairs. I think we both noticed at the same time.

"It's a parking lot," I said. "It's public."

"It's a cop parking lot. Go loiter somewhere with better snacks."

I didn't leave. She didn't tell me to again. With Reyes, I was starting to learn, that counts as an invitation.

"You closed it fast," I said.

"I didn't close anything. I don't get a vote." She finally looked at me. There was something tired under the usual tired. A second layer she usually kept better hidden. "You understand how this works, right? Small department. Twenty-some years of Boone Lockhart running it exactly how he likes it. And me, the transfer, fourteen months in, still getting asked where I'm *really* from like that's a real question." She picked up the coffee, looked into it, and set it down without drinking. It had a skin on top. It had been sitting there a while. "I flagged things. They're in a report. The report was read for exactly as long as it took somebody to decide this town didn't need an open case going into the holidays."

"What did you flag?"

"That's not something I'm going to discuss with a sixteen-year-old in a parking lot."

"You asked Delgado for the Fuel Stop footage," I said. "Wednesday morning. And then somebody told you to call him back and say never mind."

Reyes went very still.

Not dramatic. Small. Controlled. The kind of stillness you'd miss if you hadn't spent six years learning to read adults for the exact second they stop performing calm.

"How do you know that?"

"Because I went and got it before it was erased," I said. "Sunday. It records over itself every seven days. Monday night was gone on Monday."

She looked at me for a long time. Then she looked at the building behind her, the department's side door, the windows. Then back at me.

"Get in the truck," she said.

* * *

We sat in the cab with the doors shut and the engine off. The windows started fogging almost right away. Reyes's truck smelled like old coffee and the pine air freshener hanging from the mirror, which had stopped smelling like pine probably years ago.

I showed her everything on my phone. I'd made clips, the way Des showed me. 11:26, the small car with the dim headlight going out toward the point. 11:38, Camille walking, both shoes on. 11:39, the hooded figure from the left, the stiff right leg, the forty seconds on the shoulder of the road, the brake lights at the edge of the frame. 12:16, the small car coming back.

4:58 AM. The small car going out again.

5:23 AM. Coming back.

Reyes watched all of it without saying a word. When it was done she held out her hand, and I gave her the phone, and she watched the 4:58 clip three more times by herself.

"Who else has this?" she said.

"Des Choi. And Delgado kept a copy of the whole night. In his desk drawer. He said he'd give it to anybody with a badge who asked him nicely."

"Smart." She said it like she meant it. "That's the original, then. That's the one that matters. Yours is a copy of a copy. A defense lawyer would eat yours for breakfast." She handed me back the phone. "But Delgado's isn't."

"So you can use it."

"I can't do anything with it. The case is closed." She rubbed her eyes. "But that's not the same thing as nothing."

She was quiet for a while. Rain started, the thin Quiet Hollow kind that's more like the air deciding to be wet. It ticked on the roof of the truck.

"I'm going to tell you some things," she said. "And you're going to understand that this conversation didn't happen."

"Okay."

"I mean that, Wren. If this conversation comes back to me, I lose my job, and then there's nobody in that building who's going to look at any of this ever again." She waited until I nodded. "The shoe you found. The left one outside the tape. I logged it. I logged it because you told me about it, and because it was sitting upright with the laces tied, and that isn't how things land when people fall. It's in evidence. The ME's report mentions it in one line."

"And the right one?"

"Never found. We looked. Brennan and two volunteer firemen walked the rocks at low tide. Nothing." She tapped the steering wheel. "The ocean takes things. That's what the report says."

"The right shoe was on her at 11:40. It's on the camera."

"I know. Now." She looked at me sideways. "There's something else. On the railing. At the top. Right where the rust is worst, where the bars are broken out. There was a smear on it. Fresh. Reddish brown on reddish brown, you'd miss it unless you were standing exactly where I was standing with the light coming from exactly where it was coming from."

"Blood?"

"I swabbed it. It's sitting in a lab in Portland in a queue labeled *non-priority*. Because there's no case. And Camille's injuries were all at the bottom. Not at the top." She didn't look at me. "So either it's old, from some tourist who cut themselves on that railing last summer. Or somebody else bled on that railing very recently."

The rain ticked on the roof. I thought about the 4:58 car. Twenty-five minutes out there in the dark.

"Somebody went back," I said.

"Maybe." Reyes's voice was careful. "Maybe somebody went back. Or maybe it's a fisherman who couldn't sleep, and a tourist with a cut hand, and a kid in a hoodie who has a bad knee and a reason to lie about where he was. And you're stringing them together because you want them to be a story."

"Do you think that's what I'm doing?"

She thought about it. I liked that she thought about it.

"No," she said finally. "I think you're doing what I'd be doing if anybody would let me."

* * *

She reached into the center console and pulled out a business card. Plain. Department letterhead. She turned it over and wrote a number on the back with a pen she found in the cupholder. Not the number on the front.

"This isn't me deputizing you," she said, holding it out. "I want that to be extremely clear. You are sixteen. You are not a detective. You do not have a badge or training or a lawyer, and nobody is going to protect you if this goes sideways. This is me admitting that I have a stack of things I can't officially look at. And you apparently have a habit of finding things that don't officially exist yet."

I took the card. "So we're working together."

"We are absolutely not working together. If anyone asks, I've met you at one crime scene and one parking lot, and neither of those conversations was interesting." She tapped the card in my hand. "But if you find something real, something with a shape to it, not a hunch, not a vibe, that number goes to my phone and nowhere else. You don't post it. You don't put it in a group chat. You don't tell the Beacon."

"I wouldn't tell the Beacon."

"Priya Malhotra was in the library with you on Monday," Reyes said. "For forty minutes. In the archive."

I stared at her.

"Small town," she said. "Ms. Feiler and I have coffee on Thursdays." She looked at me for a long moment, and her face changed. Got more careful. "Can I ask you something?"

"Okay."

"Who knows you're asking about that jacket?"

I started counting. Des. Priya. Ms. Feiler. Delgado, sort of. Gary Ostrander, who'd probably told his whole bowling league. Theo, since Tuesday, at his locker.

And a kitchen full of people at Camille's mom's house. Everybody, Priya had said. Half the women in Quiet Hollow.

I told Reyes. All of it. She listened, and her face got more careful with each name.

"That's too many people, Wren," she said.

* * *

That night the rain got serious. I lay in bed and listened to it on the roof and the gutters overflowing by my window, a sound I've fallen asleep to my whole life.

I didn't fall asleep to it that night.

I was thinking about Theo's face at his locker. The color going out of it. The way he'd looked at his phone, like he was checking whether something was still there.

I was thinking about the brake lights at the edge of the frame. Somebody waiting for him.

I was thinking about a small dark car with one tired headlight, driving out to the edge of the world twice in one night.

At 11:14, my phone lit up on the nightstand.

A number I didn't know. No name. No area code I recognized. No profile picture. Just a text bubble on my lock screen, glowing in the dark room.

Four words.

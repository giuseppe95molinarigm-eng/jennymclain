# Chapter Fourteen

We met in my garage on Saturday morning. All three of us. It felt like enough of an event that my dad stuck his head in from the kitchen, looked at us, and said, "Should I be charging admission?"

"Dad."

"I'm going to the hardware store," he said. "Then I'm going to the mill to pick up my check. Then I'm coming home." He looked at Priya, who was sitting very straight on the far end of the old couch. "You're Amrita's niece."

"Yes, sir."

"I'm sorry about your cousin."

"Thank you."

He nodded. He looked at the whiteboard, which was facing the room for the first time, covered in green marker and arrows. He looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at me.

"I'm coming home," he said again. Like a promise. Then he left.

Priya watched the door close. "He's nice," she said, sounding almost surprised.

"He's trying."

Des was sprawled on the other end of the couch like nothing had ever happened. That's Des. He doesn't hold onto tension any better than he holds onto his phone charger. Priya sat on her end with her arms crossed. Not hostile. Just careful, the way you're careful around a bruise that's healing but still tender.

"Okay," she said, opening her laptop like this was a meeting she'd scheduled and not a truce she was still deciding whether to honor. "Walk me through it again. Slowly. I want to actually understand it this time, instead of just reacting to the sound of your sister's name."

So I walked her through it. The instant photo. *Hand-me-down day.* The new jacket on Rowan, the cuff on the arm behind her. Two jackets, bought new, one given away. The person who'd stayed in Quiet Hollow for six years while everyone around them grew up and scattered.

Des was nodding halfway through. "That tracks. That's not you reaching. That's a real gap in the logic, and it only closes one way."

Priya was quiet for a long time, turning it over.

"Fine," she said finally. "I hate how much sense that makes." She looked at the whiteboard. "So where do we even start looking for a six-year-old receipt?"

"Kessler's Supply," I said.

"Because it's the only place in town."

"Because it's the only place in town that's ever sold anything like that jacket. Everybody's mom bought their rain gear there. If it was a particular style, somebody might remember. Or at least there might be paper on it somewhere."

"Places like Kessler's don't exactly have their inventory in the cloud, Wren."

"No," I said. "Which is why there might still be paper."

* * *

Des found the jacket before we left my garage.

Kessler's had a website once. Somebody's nephew built it about ten years ago, nobody ever updated it, and eventually it went dead. But dead websites don't really die either. There are archives that save old copies of web pages, like a time machine you can scroll through. Des typed in Kessler's old address, picked a snapshot from six years ago, and clicked around.

"There," he said.

A product page. A grainy photo of a jacket on a hanger against a white wall.

[[LOG]]
HARBORLINE Women's Canvas Chore Coat
Navy. 12 oz. duck canvas. Red/black buffalo check flannel lining.
Sizes XS–XL. $89.99
*Limited stock — ask about special orders!*
[[/LOG]]

The three of us looked at it for a long time without saying anything.

"Women's," Priya said.

"Women's small," I said. "If it was too small on Camille."

Des clicked through more snapshots. The jacket was there in the spring snapshot from six years ago. It was gone by the next spring. Gone from the website, gone from the store.

"So it was only sold for like a year," Des said. "Maybe less."

"And somebody bought two."

* * *

Kessler's smells the way it's smelled my whole life. Canvas and rubber boots and the wax they put on the wood floor, which is older than any of us. There's a bell over the door that's been ringing since before my dad was born.

The man behind the counter was maybe sixty, with a gray mustache and a name tag that said DARRYL. He had the face of someone who's spent forty years in a small town learning exactly how much of everybody's business to know and how much to mind.

My dad had been in that morning for something. Darryl was still holding the receipt. He looked at me and said, "Your dad just left."

"I know. I'm here for something else."

I did the reputation thing. It usually gets me either an eye-roll or a surprising amount of help, depending on who's behind the counter. "I'm trying to find out about a jacket you used to sell. A HarborLine chore coat. Women's. Navy, with a plaid lining. Probably about six years ago."

Darryl looked at the three of us for a long moment, the way adults look at teenagers asking strange questions, deciding if they're a nuisance or something real.

"You're the Ashby girl," he said.

"I am."

"Your dad's been in here more this month than all of last year." Something in his face shifted, not unkindly. "Deadbolt a couple Saturdays ago. Weatherstripping today. Says the house is getting drafty." He paused. "I remember your sister. Nice kid. Talked my ear off about a camping trip she was planning. Bought hiking boots for it." He said it easily. The way the whole town says her name sometimes, like she's a fact of local geography instead of a person who's missing. "What's this about?"

"I don't want to say more than I know yet," I said. "But I think it matters. I think it's connected to something real."

He looked at Priya then, for a long beat. I watched him place her. "You're Camille Malhotra's cousin."

Priya didn't flinch. "Yes."

"She bought a pair of rain boots here. In the spring. Yellow ones. Said they made her feel like a duck." He was quiet for a moment. "I'm sorry for your family."

"Thank you," Priya said.

Darryl sighed, the long sigh of a man who'd rather not be involved in anything but couldn't stand the idea of not helping when a kid clearly wasn't messing around.

"HarborLine. I remember that coat. Good coat. They quit making it." He glanced at the clock over the register. "Before the new register went in, we wrote everything on carbon-copy tickets. Special orders especially. Name on the ticket so we know who to call when it comes in. Boxes of the old ones are in the back room. My accountant'd have my head if I threw them out." He looked at us. "I close at six. You want to go digging through six years of paper, you've got until then."

* * *

It took us until almost four.

The back room was a long, low space with a single bulb and shelves of cardboard boxes labeled in black marker by year and month. Darryl filed by month, not by item, which meant we needed the right season. The coat had been on the website the spring of that year, and Rowan had worn it new on the Fourth of July.

"So spring to early July," Des said. "April, May, June."

Three boxes. Hundreds of tickets in each, carbon copies gone soft and gray, written in Darryl's handwriting and two or three other people's. Rain boots. Fishing line. Work gloves. A whole lot of WD-40.

We sat on the floor and split them up. It was slow. The kind of tedium that makes your eyes blur after the first hour. Des kept reading things out loud to stay awake. "Someone named Gus bought eleven mouse traps in May. *Eleven*, Wren. What happened to Gus." Priya told him to focus. He said he was focusing on Gus.

Priya found it.

She didn't say anything at first. She just held the ticket up and turned it around so Des and I could see it, and her face was doing something complicated.

[[LOG]]
KESSLER'S SUPPLY — SPECIAL ORDER
Date: 6/14
Item: HarborLine W's Canvas Chore Coat — NAVY — SM
Qty: 2  (1 in stock, 1 ordered)
Price: 89.99 ea.
PAID: CASH
HOLD FOR: N. PETRAKIS
Called 6/21 — picked up 6/22
[[/LOG]]

June 14. Six years ago. Two weeks before the Fourth of July.

Two jackets.

N. Petrakis.

"That's—" Priya's voice came out smaller than I'd ever heard it. "That's Nora. That's my aunt's friend. She babysat me and Camille both, when we were little. She's basically family." Her hand holding the ticket had started shaking, very slightly. "She was at the funeral, Wren. She held my aunt up the whole service. She brought a casserole every day for a week. I hugged her."

Something cold settled in my stomach. The specific cold of a thought you'd waved away coming back to stand in front of you.

"I thought about her," I said. "For a second. On Tuesday morning. When I was trying to picture who it could be. She used to watch me and Rowan on Saturdays. I didn't even write her name down. It felt so random."

"It wasn't random," Des said quietly. "You were pattern-matching. You just didn't know you were doing it. That's sort of your whole thing."

* * *

Darryl came back to check on us. He saw our faces and slowed down. He looked at the ticket over Priya's shoulder.

"Petrakis," he said. He frowned at it. "Sure. Nora. Comes in a few times a year. That's my writing." He took it from her, gently, and held it up to the bulb. "Two smalls. I remember that, actually. We only had the one on the rack. She wanted two of the same. Said she always buys two of anything she really loves, because then when one wears out you're not heartbroken." He shook his head. "Funny thing to remember."

He started to hand it back. Then he stopped.

"Come to think of it," he said slowly, "she was in here asking about that coat again. This spring."

The three of us went very still.

"April, I want to say. Early April. Asked if we could still order the HarborLine coat in navy. Small. I told her they hadn't made it in years. She asked if I could find one anywhere, any store, online, didn't matter what it cost." Darryl rubbed his mustache. "I looked. Nothing. She seemed real disappointed. Said it was for a birthday. Said she'd figure something else out."

"April," Priya said. Her voice was very flat.

"Early April."

"Camille's birthday is April ninth." She looked at me. "She turned seventeen on April ninth."

Nobody said anything.

"She couldn't get a new one," I said slowly. "So she gave Camille her own."

Priya closed her eyes.

* * *

Darryl let us take a photo of the ticket. Then he put it in an envelope and wrote the date on it, and sealed it, and put it in the safe behind the register, which I hadn't known Kessler's had.

"If anybody official comes asking," he said, "it's in there. And I'll tell them who found it." He looked at us over the counter. "Now I'd appreciate it if you three didn't burn my store down chasing whatever this is."

"We won't," I said.

"I've heard about you," Darryl said. "I've heard about the Petersons' cat."

* * *

We sat in Des's car in the Kessler's lot afterward, the photo of that faded carbon ticket glowing on three separate phones. Nobody said much. The light was going gold and long, the way it does in November right before it gets dark, which in November is about four-thirty.

"It doesn't mean anything," Priya said finally. "Not by itself. A receipt from six years ago. A woman bought two coats because she likes buying two coats. She gave one to Rowan because Rowan was cold. She gave the other one to Camille for her birthday. That's—" She stopped. "That's nice. That's a nice thing to do. That's exactly the kind of thing Nora does."

"It is," I said.

"So it doesn't mean anything."

"It means Camille was wrong about the jacket," I said. "It means Camille went up to Hollow Point thinking she was wearing Rowan's jacket, the one from the poster. And the person who gave it to her could have explained that in one sentence."

Priya turned to look at me from the passenger seat. "Explain what?"

"*I bought two.*" I looked at the ticket on my screen. "If you were Nora, and Camille came to you saying *I know you had Rowan Ashby's jacket, what did you do to her*, all you'd have to say is I bought two. Here's the receipt. Ask Darryl." I looked up. "So why didn't she?"

"We don't know she didn't."

"Then why hasn't she said anything since? To anybody. She knows I've been asking about the jacket. Your whole family's kitchen knows. If I were Nora and I'd bought two matching jackets and given one to each girl and I loved them both, and then one of them died wearing it, and the sister of the other one started asking questions about it." My voice had gotten tight. "I'd call. I'd come over. I'd sit in my kitchen and say *honey, I know what you're thinking, I bought two.*"

"She did sit in your kitchen," Des said quietly. "On the Tuesday. With the casserole."

The car was very quiet.

"And she asked your dad if you'd seen anything," Des said. "Up close."

Priya was staring straight ahead at the Kessler's sign. Her jaw was tight. "Wren, I've had dinner at that woman's house. She called Camille sweetheart. She cried at the vigil harder than almost anybody."

"I know."

"I need you to understand what you're asking me to think about right now."

"I do understand. I'm not asking you to be okay with it. I'm asking you to help me find out if it's true. Because if it is, she doesn't get to just be the woman who cried at the vigil. She has to be whatever she actually is."

Priya sat with that for a long time. When she finally turned back to me, her eyes were wet, but her voice had gone steady in the way that meant she'd decided something.

"Okay," she said. "Then we find out properly. Not a hunch. Not a six-year-old receipt that could still mean nothing. I want an actual reason before I let myself believe this about someone who held me at my cousin's funeral."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair."

Des started the car. "Where am I driving?"

Priya was quiet for a second. Then she said, "Larch Street. Sixty-two Larch. It's on the way to my house anyway."

* * *

Larch Street is in the old part of town, up the hill from the harbor, where the houses are small and close together and have porches. It was almost dark by the time we turned onto it. Some of the porch lights were already on.

Sixty-two was a little gray house with white trim. A wreath on the door made of fake fall leaves. A welcome mat. Window boxes with chrysanthemums in them, the dark red kind. It looked like a house in a commercial for insurance.

The driveway was empty.

Des slowed down as we passed. None of us said anything. We just looked. A lamp on in the front window. Framed photos on the wall inside, just visible through the curtains. A porch swing.

We were almost to the end of the block when headlights came around the corner ahead of us.

A small car. Dark green, I'd find out later, though in the dusk it just looked dark. A wagon. Boxy. The kind of car half the town drives.

It came toward us slowly, the way you drive on a street you live on. And as it passed under the streetlight, I saw it.

One headlight bright. One headlight dim. Yellow and tired. Like an eye half closed.

My whole body went cold.

"Des," I said. Very quietly.

"I see it," he said, just as quietly. His hands had tightened on the wheel. "I see it."

Priya didn't say anything. She was looking out her window.

The car passed us. In the side mirror I watched it slow down and turn into the driveway of number sixty-two. The dim headlight swept across the white trim and the window boxes and went out.

The driver's door opened.

Nora Petrakis got out, holding a grocery bag, wearing a soft gray cardigan, her reading glasses pushed up on her head. She was turning toward her porch when she saw Des's car.

She stopped.

She looked straight at us. At Des, at the wheel. At me, in the back. At Priya, in the passenger seat, closest to her.

Then she smiled, a big warm smile, and lifted her free hand and waved.

Her right hand.

Priya waved back. I don't think she meant to. I think it was thirteen years of reflex.

Des kept driving. He didn't speed up. He didn't slow down. He drove to the end of the block, and turned the corner, and pulled over in front of somebody else's house, and turned off the car, and none of us said anything for a very long time.

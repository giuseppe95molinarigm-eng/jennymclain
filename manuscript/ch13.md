# Chapter Thirteen

I woke up at 6:58 with my face mashed into my pillow and the sentence already in my head, like it had been sitting there all night waiting for me to open my eyes.

*hand-me-down day.*

I lay there, not moving. Running it back the way I'd learned to run everything back these past few weeks. Slow. Looking for the piece that didn't fit, instead of the piece that felt good.

Rowan's caption on her last post. *In my favorite hand-me-down. Never giving this back.*

The Sharpie on the instant photo. *Hand-me-down day!!*

You don't call something a hand-me-down if you bought it. You don't call it that if a boyfriend gave it to you either. A boyfriend gives you a hoodie, and you call it his hoodie, and you wear it until you break up and then you burn it in a sink. A hand-me-down is something that was somebody else's first. Somebody older. Somebody who'd already had it, already worn it, and passed it down.

Except the jacket in the instant photo wasn't old.

That's what I'd seen last night without seeing it. The canvas was dark, almost black. The collar was stiff, not flipped yet. It hung off her shoulders in big square folds like it had just come out of a bag. That jacket had never been worn by anyone before the Fourth of July.

So it wasn't a hand-me-down in the regular sense. It was a joke. Rowan being Rowan. Somebody older had handed her a brand-new jacket, and she'd called it a hand-me-down, because the person who gave it to her was the kind of person who hands you things. Like an aunt. Like a neighbor. Like a grown-up.

I sat up. Something else was nagging at me, under that. Something in the corner of the picture.

A dark sleeve with the cuff turned back. Holding a paper plate.

I was out of bed and down the hall before I'd decided to go.

* * *

The box was where we'd left it. I didn't turn on the lamp this time. I sat on the floor of Rowan's room in the gray light from the window and dug out the Fourth of July photo and held it up close to my face.

Then I took a picture of it with my phone and zoomed in, because the phone could see better than I could.

The edge of the frame. Out of focus, smeared by the firelight. Somebody standing behind Rowan and a little to the left, mostly cut off. A shoulder. An arm bent at the elbow. A hand holding a paper plate with what looked like half a hot dog on it. A wrist, thin. The sleeve was dark, and stiff, and the cuff was folded back once, the way you fold back a cuff when a sleeve is a little too long.

And the folded-back part of the cuff, where the inside of the sleeve showed, was checked.

Red and black.

I sat on the floor with the phone in my hand and the instant photo in the other and looked back and forth between them until I was sure. I was sure. There was no version of that cuff that was anything else.

Two jackets.

There were two of them. In the same picture. On the same night. One on my sister, brand new, hanging off her shoulders like a present. And one on somebody standing behind her eating a hot dog.

* * *

I've tried to explain to Des what it feels like when a case turns over. He says it sounds like a panic attack. It's not, exactly. It's more like when you've been looking at one of those optical illusions, the vase that's also two faces, and you've only ever been able to see the vase. Then something shifts, and you see the faces, and you can never go back to just seeing the vase again.

I'd been looking at the vase for three weeks.

The vase was this: the jacket on Camille was Rowan's jacket. The one on the poster. *Last seen wearing.* Somebody had kept it for six years, and somebody had given it to Camille, and Camille had figured it out, and gone to the archive and asked whether they ever found the jacket. And then she'd gone up to Hollow Point to confront whoever had it, because she thought she was wearing a missing girl's clothes.

Camille had believed that. I had believed that. All of it built on one thing.

That there was only one jacket.

But there were two. Bought new, at the same time, by somebody who wore one and handed the other to a cold seventeen-year-old at a bonfire.

So the jacket on Camille might not be Rowan's at all. It might be the other one. The twin. The one on the arm with the paper plate.

Which meant Rowan's jacket was still missing.

Which meant whatever happened to my sister, the jacket had gone with her, the way the poster said, and it had never come back. Nobody had been keeping it in a closet for six years. Nobody had dressed Camille in it.

I sat with that for a while, because it hurt in a way I wasn't expecting. Some part of me had been hoping. If somebody had Rowan's jacket, then somebody knew where it had been. Somebody had touched it after. Somebody could be found and asked.

If it was a twin, there was no thread. Just a matching jacket on a matching night, and my sister in hers, walking off a beach into nothing.

And then, under the hurt, the other thing. The faces, after the vase.

Camille had died believing something that wasn't true.

She'd gone up to that lighthouse to accuse somebody of something about Rowan. Something big enough to make her turn off her location and walk up those stairs in the dark, alone, when she was scared of that place. And if the jacket was the twin, then whoever she'd gone up there to accuse had been holding the other half of a pair the whole time.

Whoever it was could have explained it in one sentence. *I bought two.*

Maybe they had. Maybe they'd tried.

Something had still happened on that grass.

He's right. I do.

I read it back twice.

Then I sat there and tried to put faces to the questions. That's the part I'd been avoiding, because I knew how it would go. You start picturing people, and you start picturing people you know, and then you're the girl in town who thinks the youth pastor did it.

Coaches stay for years. But coaches belong to specific sports and specific seasons. They don't drift between a seventeen-year-old's friend group and a completely different one six years later. Teachers, same problem, mostly. Unless it was somebody who'd been teaching long enough to have had both of them, which felt like a stretch.

And then there's the category all by itself. The family friends. The women who show up at your family's worst week with a casserole and don't leave until the dishes are done. The ones who babysat you when you were seven and still send your dad a Christmas card. The ones so woven into everybody's houses that nobody ever thinks to ask what they're doing there, because they've always just been there.

I thought, for about one second, of Nora.

She used to watch me and Rowan on Saturdays. Grilled cheese cut in triangles. Letting us stay up for the end of the movie. Two weeks ago she'd brought us a casserole and hugged me at the vigil and called me honey. I'd stood at Camille's funeral reception and watched her run the Malhotras' kitchen like it was her own.

I didn't stay on the thought. There wasn't any reason to. Nora was just the shape my brain reached for when I pictured that kind of person. She'd been folded into half the households in this town at one point or another. So had a dozen other women I could've named if I'd sat there long enough. Mrs. Okafor. The lady who runs the church nursery. Half the moms on the PTA.

I didn't write any of their names down. A feeling isn't a suspect. I'd already learned that the expensive way this month, with Priya.

What I wrote down instead was the next step. Because the shape of it mattered more than any one name.

*Find the store.*

* * *

I couldn't do this alone. I'd spent the last week finding that out one person at a time, and I wasn't willing to lose the last two people I had over being proud.

I texted Priya first. It felt like the harder one. Harder felt like the one I owed first.

I wrote it four times. I deleted it four times. Every version sounded like an argument. Like a lawyer. In the end I stopped trying to win anything.

[[LOG]]

me: I'm sending you everything. my whole case log, from the first morning. the camera, the 5 AM car, Theo's photos, the break-in at my house during the funeral. all of it. you can read it or delete it.
me: and then one more thing. I found something last night. it changes the jacket. I think Camille was wrong about what she found. I think she died believing something that wasn't true.
me: I'm not asking you to forget anything. I'm asking if there's a version of you that still wants to find out what happened to her. because I think I finally know where to look, and I don't want to look there without you.
[[/LOG]]

Then I exported the whole channel. Every timestamp, every note. Every stupid thing I'd thought at two in the morning. Even the part where I'd written that Priya told a kitchen full of people. Even the part about the funeral.

I sent it before I could think about how it was going to look.

The dots appeared almost right away. Then disappeared. Then appeared again. From anybody else that would mean nothing. From Priya it meant drafting and deleting, choosing every word the way she chooses everything.

It took eleven minutes.

* * *

My dad was already downstairs. I could hear the coffee maker going and the radio on low, a man reading the tide tables in the same voice he's read them in for thirty years. 6:12 high, 12:40 low.

He was standing at the counter when I came in. He looked at me. I looked at him. Neither of us said anything about the night before. But he poured a second cup of coffee without asking and slid it across the counter to me, and I'm not allowed to drink coffee, and I drank it.

"You're doing the face," he said.

"Which one?"

"The one where you know something." He looked into his own mug. "Rowan used to do it too. Same face. Exact same."

I didn't know what to say to that. He didn't seem to need me to say anything.

I took my coffee upstairs. I looked at the board on my laptop for a while longer, at the one word I'd written at the bottom, in capitals, by itself.

*STORE.*

Somewhere in Quiet Hollow, two navy jackets had come off a shelf, six years ago. Somebody had carried them up to a register. Somebody had rung them up. Maybe there was a receipt, or a ledger, or a clerk who remembered the person who'd bought two of the exact same thing on an ordinary afternoon without any idea what it would mean.

I just had to find it before whoever had been in my house decided a text message hadn't been enough.

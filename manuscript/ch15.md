# Chapter Fifteen

I didn't sleep Saturday night either. But at least this time I spent it doing something useful.

By four in the morning I had the whole thing on my laptop. Not the shape of a theory anymore. A list. Everything I'd seen in three and a half weeks, sorted. And I'd found the thing that made my hands go cold on the keyboard.

I'd used the same words twice.

I scrolled back through my case log to the first morning. 7:46 AM, sitting on my bed with my running shoes still on. *Smelled like salt and something else. wood? cedar maybe?* And then, later, writing it out properly, the way I'd described it to Des in the kitchen. *Something dry and woody. Like the inside of an old dresser drawer.*

Then I made myself go back to the vigil. Not to anything I'd written down. I hadn't opened the app that night. Just to the moment itself, Nora's arms around me in the cold blue light, and the words my head had used for it while it was happening.

*Dryer sheets, and something woody underneath. Like the inside of an old dresser drawer.*

The same words. Not similar words. The exact same ones. My brain had reached for the same sentence for the same smell, two days apart, without ever telling me why.

I'd filed it under things that are probably nothing.

I sat there in the dark with the laptop on my knees and the rain on the window, and I made the board.

I looked at it until the screen dimmed. Then I tapped it awake and looked at it some more.

*She's the one who watched us on Saturdays.*

I'd written that sentence myself, in my own head, three weeks ago, standing in my kitchen listening to Des open the garage. *The only people who knew it were me, my dad, Rowan, and whoever was watching us on Saturdays.* And then, last week, standing in my garage looking at a door that had been left two inches up, I'd made a list of everybody who knew the trick. Me. My dad. Des. Rowan.

I'd left her off.

I'd left her off because she wasn't a person to me. She was a casserole. She was grilled cheese cut in triangles. She was a kind of furniture, like the clock that runs four minutes fast. You don't put the clock on a suspect list.

That's the whole trick, I'd told myself, the first morning on the cliff stairs. Paying attention. Nobody wants to hear it because it sounds too easy.

I hadn't been paying attention. I'd been paying attention to everybody except the one person who'd been standing in my kitchen.

* * *

I called Reyes at nine on Sunday morning, which I figured was late enough to count as civilized.

She listened to all of it. The ticket. Darryl's April. The headlight on Larch Street. The smell, the hand, the garage door, the funeral. She didn't interrupt once. When I was done there was a long silence on the line, and I could hear her breathing, and a kettle starting to whistle somewhere behind her.

"Okay," she said. "That's different."

"So you can—"

"Wait. Listen to me. That's different, and it's not enough." The kettle stopped. "Tell me what you'd say to a judge. Not to me. To a judge who's never met you, who's had coffee with Boone Lockhart every Tuesday for twenty years, and who has to sign a piece of paper that lets me walk into a forty-eight-year-old dental receptionist's house and go through her closet."

I opened my mouth.

"A receipt from six years ago," Reyes said. "For two coats. Perfectly legal. A smell, described by a sixteen-year-old, in her own diary. A bandage on a hand. A headlight on grainy footage with no plate, and I promise you there are forty cars in this county with a dim left headlight, because it's the Pacific Northwest and it rains eleven months a year and everybody's wiring is half rotted. A woman who left a funeral reception to get ice. A woman who knows how to open a garage door she opened every Saturday for years." She paused. "Do you hear it?"

"I hear it."

"Every piece of it is real. I believe every piece of it is real. And every piece of it, by itself, has an innocent explanation. A lawyer would make each one an innocent explanation, one at a time, until a jury forgot they were ever all in a row." She breathed out. "And the worst part. You don't have a why. You told me yourself. Why would she meet Camille at midnight? A judge is going to ask me that. I don't have an answer."

"Camille thought Nora had Rowan's jacket."

"So Camille confronts her, and Nora says *I bought two*, and that's the end of it. That's not a motive, Wren. That's a misunderstanding."

I didn't say anything. She was right. It was the exact thing I'd said to Priya in the car.

"Here's what I'm going to do," Reyes said. "I'm going to drive past Larch Street on my way to the grocery store, like a normal person who needs eggs, and I'm going to write down a plate. I'm going to call the lab in Portland again and be annoying about a swab. And I'm going to think." Her voice changed. "And here's what you're going to do. Nothing. You are not going near Nora Petrakis. You are not going to her house. You are not going to call her or text her or *bump into her* at the post office. If you're right about any of this, she's already been in your house once. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Say it like you mean it longer than six seconds."

I almost laughed. My dad had been talking to her.

"I won't go near Nora," I said.

That was true. I didn't.

I went near Theo instead.

* * *

It was Priya's idea. That part matters to me.

She called me Sunday afternoon. I'd sent her the suspect board. She'd been quiet for hours, which I'd understood. When she called, her voice was raw, like she'd been crying and then stopped and then started again.

"I keep thinking about the vigil," she said. "You wrote that she looked at Theo. Once. When he came in."

"Yes."

"I was standing right next to her. I didn't notice." Her breath shook. "And I keep thinking about Theo. At the funeral. Sitting all the way in the back. He didn't come to the house. He didn't call my aunt. He was her *boyfriend*, Wren. He was at my aunt's Fourth of July barbecue. He ate three plates of her biryani and said it was the best thing he ever had. And then she dies and he doesn't even send a text." She was quiet for a second. "He's scared. He's not sad, he's scared. I've been watching him for three weeks and I didn't know what I was watching."

"He knows something."

"He knows everything," Priya said. "That's what I think. I think he knows everything and he's been carrying it around by himself, and he's seventeen, and it's eating him alive." Her voice hardened. "And I think he'll talk to me. I don't think he'll talk to you. I don't think he'll talk to Reyes. But he owes me. He owes Camille. And I'm the closest thing to Camille he has left."

"He's going to see me and leave," I said.

"Maybe." Priya put her phone face down on her knee. "Then he leaves. And we try something else." She looked at me. "But you don't say anything until I say you can. You don't do the face. You don't do the detective voice. You sit there."

"I don't have a detective voice."

"You have like three of them," Priya said.

* * *

Ms. Feiler let us into the archive at 2:55. She didn't ask why. She just unlocked it, looked at the two of us, and said, "I'll be at the front desk. I'll be at the front desk for a long time." Then she left, and pulled the door almost closed behind her.

Theo came at 3:04.

He opened the door and saw Priya at the microfilm reader, and then he saw me sitting on the step stool by the yearbook shelves, and he stopped. His hand stayed on the doorknob. I watched his weight shift toward the hall.

"You said just you," he said to Priya.

"I didn't say just me. I said I needed to talk to you." Priya didn't get up. "Close the door, Theo."

He didn't close it.

"Please," she said.

Something about the way she said it. He closed the door. He stood with his back against it, knee brace locked straight, both hands jammed in the front pocket of his hoodie. The same hoodie. I knew it was the same hoodie. I'd watched it walk back into the dark eleven times.

He looked worse than he had at his locker. Thinner, if that's possible in three weeks. His eyes had a raw, scraped look, like he'd been rubbing them.

"I already talked to the detective," he said. "My lawyer said I don't have to talk to anybody."

"I'm not anybody," Priya said. "I'm Cam's cousin."

He didn't say anything to that.

"Theo." Priya's voice was very calm. I'd never heard her that calm. It was the calm of someone holding a glass full to the brim. "I know you met her on the road by the Fuel Stop. At 11:40. There's a camera."

His face did nothing for a second. Then it did everything at once.

"I didn't hurt her," he said. It came out of him fast, like he'd been holding it in his mouth for three weeks. "I didn't. I swear to God, Priya, I didn't touch her, I talked to her for like a minute and she told me to go back and I *went back*. I didn't follow her. I couldn't even follow her, look at me—" He gestured at his knee, almost angrily. "I can't even walk up a *driveway*."

"I know," Priya said. "I know you didn't hurt her. I've seen it. You walked away from her."

He stared at her.

"I'm not here because I think you hurt her," Priya said. "I'm here because I think somebody else did. And I think you know who she went up there to meet."

The room was very quiet. The microfilm reader hummed.

Theo's eyes went to me. Just for a second. Then back to Priya.

"I can't," he said.

That's when I broke the rule. I didn't plan to. It just came out, quiet, from the step stool.

"Nora Petrakis," I said.

* * *

I've thought a lot about what happened to his face. I've decided I don't want to describe it exactly. It was private. It was the face of a seventeen-year-old boy finding out that the worst thing he'd been carrying around wasn't a secret anymore.

He slid down the door until he was sitting on the floor, his bad leg stuck straight out in front of him. He put his face in his hands.

"How do you know," he said into them. "How do you *know*."

Priya looked at me. I shook my head. Not yet.

"Theo," Priya said gently. "Tell me what Nora is to you."

He told us.

He told us looking at the floor, in a voice so flat and quiet I had to lean forward to hear it. It had started last December. Almost a year ago. He'd been sixteen. His mom worked late shifts in Astoria and his dad was gone a lot, and Nora had been at their house helping with his grandmother when she was sick, and she'd been nice to him. She'd listened. She'd asked about soccer, about college, about whether he was okay. Nobody else asked him if he was okay. And then it was more than that.

He kept calling it *a relationship*. He kept saying *we*. He said it like somebody had taught him to say it, carefully, the way you say a word in another language. *We had to be careful. We couldn't tell anybody because people wouldn't understand.*

I didn't say anything. I didn't know what to say. But I want to be honest about what I thought, sitting there on that step stool.

He'd been sixteen. She was forty-eight. She'd been in his house helping with his dying grandmother.

That's not a *we*.

Priya had gone very pale. Her hands were folded tight in her lap.

"The number," I said. "T. Was that hers?"

He nodded without looking up. "She got a different phone. Just for me. She said to save it as T. She said if anybody ever saw it, it'd look like a note to myself." He laughed, horribly. "T for Theo. Like I was texting myself. She thought of everything."

"And Camille found it."

"She saw me get out of Nora's car." His voice cracked. "The Thursday before. Behind the dental office. Nora was driving me to PT on her lunch break because I can't drive with the knee, and my mom thinks it's so nice of her, she *thanked* her. And Cam was at the Starbucks across the lot and she saw. And she didn't say anything to me. She just—" He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "She went through my phone. Sunday night. The night before. I was asleep on her couch and she went through it, and she found T, and she must have known. She must have figured it out. She copied the number into her own phone. I didn't know until Monday night."

"Monday night," Priya said.

"I was at Colby's. And she texted me. Like eleven. She said *I know about T. I know who she is. I'm going to talk to her tonight. Don't you dare come.*" He was crying now, not hiding it. "And I panicked. I made Colby drive me. I couldn't walk there, she lives off Breaker and I knew she'd walk past the Fuel Stop, so we drove out and waited. And she came, and I got out, and I begged her. I begged her not to go. I said it was my fault, it was all my fault, just yell at me, don't go up there."

"What did she say?" Priya asked. Her voice was barely there.

Theo looked up at her for the first time.

"She said *she had Rowan Ashby's jacket, Theo.*" He looked at me then. He looked right at me. "She said, *she gave me a missing girl's jacket. Do you understand that? Do you understand what that means?* And I didn't. I didn't understand. I didn't know what she was talking about. She'd been weird about that jacket for weeks, she stopped wearing it, she kept it in a bag in her closet, she wouldn't tell me why." He shook his head. "And then she said, *go back to your stupid party, Theo*, and she walked away. And I let her. I went back to the car and Colby drove me back to his house, and I sat in the bathroom for like twenty minutes, and then I went back out and played air hockey like an idiot so nobody would ask where I'd been."

* * *

Nobody said anything for a while. The microfilm reader hummed. Somebody laughed out in the library, far away, an ordinary sound from an ordinary day.

"Theo," I said. "When did you find out she was dead?"

He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "In the morning. Nora texted me."

"What time?"

"Early. Before anybody. It said something terrible happened to Camille." He swallowed. "It said *You were at Colby's all night. You never left. Say nothing to anyone until I call you. Delete this.*" He looked at the floor. "So I did. I deleted it. And then when people started asking, I told Colby to make sure people could say I was there. He did the photo thing. He thought he was helping. He thought I was scared people would say I pushed her. I was scared of that. She *told* me to be scared of that. She said I was the last one who saw her, and if anybody found out I'd left the party, everybody would think I did it, and my whole life would be over." His voice broke completely. "She said she'd waited in the parking lot at the point for an hour and Cam never showed up. She said she gave up and went home. She said Cam must have gone out on the walkway by herself after, and slipped. She said it wasn't anybody's fault."

"Theo," I said. "What time did Nora text you that morning?"

He blinked at me. Then he pulled out his phone and scrolled, and I realized he hadn't deleted everything. He'd kept a screenshot. He'd kept it for three weeks.

He held it out. I didn't take it. I just looked.

5:47 AM.

"Gary Ostrander called 911 at 6:24," I said. "The first time anybody said anything in the junior server was 6:40. Nobody knew anything happened to Camille until after six-thirty. Nobody."

Theo stared at me.

"She texted you at 5:47," I said. "Thirty-seven minutes before anybody found her."

I watched him understand it. I watched it go all the way through him, from his face down to his hands, which started to shake so hard he dropped the phone.

"She said she wasn't there," he whispered. "She said Cam never came."

"Her car went out toward the point at 11:26," I said. "It came back at 12:16. And it went back out again at 4:58 in the morning." I said it as gently as I've ever said anything. "And it came home at 5:23. And at 5:47 she texted you."

Theo made a sound I'm not going to describe either.

Priya got up from the microfilm reader. She crossed the room and sat down on the floor next to him, against the door. She didn't touch him. She just sat there, close.

I'd thought she would hate him. I'd thought, sitting there listening, that she'd have every right. He'd lied. He'd let her family think Camille went up there alone. He'd let a town call it an accident.

But she sat down next to him like Des sits next to me.

"You were sixteen," she said quietly. "When it started. Do you understand that? You were *sixteen*, and she was a grown woman in your house."

He shook his head. He didn't understand. Or he couldn't yet.

"I didn't know she was there," he said, over and over. "I swear to God. I didn't know she was there."

"I believe you," Priya said. And her voice broke on it, all the way, finally. "I believe you, Theo."

* * *

I went out into the library and called Reyes.

Ms. Feiler was at the front desk, exactly where she'd said she'd be. She looked up at me as I passed. Whatever she saw in my face made her take off her reading glasses, fold them, and set them down very carefully on the desk.

Reyes picked up on the first ring.

"I didn't go near Nora," I said.

"Wren—"

"I need you to listen. Theo Marsh is sitting on the floor of the archive room at school with Priya Malhotra. He just told us everything." I closed my eyes. "Nora Petrakis has been in a sexual relationship with him since he was sixteen. She's T. The burner was hers. She texted him at 5:47 the morning Camille died, before anybody knew, and told him to fake his alibi. And she told him she was never at the top of the stairs."

There was a silence on the line so long I thought the call had dropped.

Then Reyes's voice came back, and it was a voice I'd never heard her use before. Very quiet. Very level. Like a door closing and locking.

"Is he safe?"

"He's with Priya."

"Is he willing to say that to me? With his parents? On the record?"

I looked back through the archive door, still open a crack. Theo was on the floor with his face in his hands. Priya was beside him, talking quietly, saying something I couldn't hear.

"I think so," I said. "I think he's been waiting three weeks for somebody to ask him the right question."

"Then here's what happens," Reyes said. "This isn't Boone's case anymore. A minor reporting sexual abuse by an adult isn't something a sheriff gets to close because it's inconvenient. That goes to the county. That goes to the district attorney, and the district attorney doesn't have coffee with anybody." I heard keys. A door. "I'm coming to the school. Don't let him leave. Don't let him call her. And Wren."

"Yes."

"You just did the thing I couldn't do," Reyes said. "You gave me the why."

# Chapter Eleven

Kenzie Ochoa lost her retainer again on Halloween.

I know that sounds like the setup to a joke. It's not. It's the most important thing that happened that week, and it happened because Kenzie Ochoa cannot keep a piece of molded plastic in her mouth for more than five consecutive days.

She found me at my locker Friday morning. She was dressed as a strawberry. Most of the junior class had dressed up as something, because the school lets you on Halloween as long as you don't bring anything shaped like a weapon. Kenzie was a strawberry with green felt leaves on her head and little black sequins sewn on for seeds, and her face was very serious.

"Wren," she said. "I'm not asking. I'm begging."

"I don't do retainers anymore."

"Fifteen dollars. Des said fifteen."

"Des is not my agent."

"He kind of is, though." She looked at me with enormous, desperate strawberry eyes. "My mom said if I lose another one, she's making me pay for the replacement out of my own money, and it's three hundred dollars, Wren. Three hundred dollars. I'll be paying for it until I die."

I almost said no. I had a lot on my mind. Then I looked at her, and I thought about who Kenzie was.

Kenzie was at Colby's on Monday night. She was in two of the photos in the background, making a peace sign. Which meant she was in Colby's server. Which meant she could see the shared album.

"Okay," I said. "Where'd you have it last?"

* * *

It took me forty minutes. It was in the pocket of her driving instructor's car. Kenzie had taken it out to eat a granola bar during her lesson on Thursday, wrapped it in a napkin, and shoved the napkin in the door pocket. The driving instructor, who is also the girls' soccer coach, found it in about two seconds when I asked him to check, and handed it over with a look of profound weariness.

Kenzie screamed when I gave it to her. People in the hallway turned around. A girl dressed as a ghost dropped her coffee.

"I love you," Kenzie said. "I actually love you. I'll Venmo you right now."

"I don't want fifteen dollars."

She stopped with her phone halfway out. "You want *more*?"

"I want something else." I lowered my voice. "Colby's shared album. From Monday night. The one pinned in his server."

Her face changed. The strawberry went careful. "Why?"

"Because I think somebody lied about when some of those photos were taken. And I need the real ones. The original files, not screenshots."

"Is this about Theo?" She looked around the hallway. "Everybody says you think Theo did something to Camille."

"I don't know what Theo did. That's why I need the photos."

Kenzie bit her lip. She was quiet long enough that I thought she was going to say no.

"Colby's been weird," she said finally. "Since that Monday. He made this whole speech in the server Tuesday night, like, *if anybody asks you anything, Theo was here the whole time, that's all you know*. And everybody was like, *yeah obviously.*" She frowned. "But I was kind of confused by that."

"Why?"

"Because he wasn't." She said it simply, like it was obvious. "Theo wasn't there the whole time. He was gone for like forty minutes. Maybe more. Colby said he was throwing up in the side yard. And I was like, okay, gross, whatever." She shifted her weight, and her felt leaves bobbed. "But Colby was my ride. And my charger was in his car, and I went out to get it. Like, around 11:30. And the car wasn't there."

I didn't breathe.

"I asked Colby about it later and he said he'd moved it because of the neighbors. Which, what neighbors? It's Colby. He doesn't care about neighbors." She shrugged, uneasily. "I didn't think about it until everybody started saying Theo was there all night. And then I thought about it a lot. But I didn't want to be the one to say something, you know? Because then it's like, *Kenzie's the reason Theo's in trouble.*"

"Kenzie," I said. "Would you say that to a detective?"

Her eyes went wide. "Like a real one?"

"Like a real one."

She looked at me for a long moment. A strawberry with a three-hundred-dollar retainer back in its case in her hand.

"Colby keeps saying people have older brothers," she said. "Do you know what Colby's older brother does? He's in the Coast Guard. In Alaska. He literally rescues people." She almost smiled. "Colby's not scary. Colby's just loud."

She pulled out her phone, opened the album, selected every photo from Monday night, and sent them all to me in one long batch, the full original files, sitting there in my messages with all their information intact.

"Don't tell anybody it was me," she said. "Unless it has to be me. Then I guess you can tell them."

"Thank you."

"You found my retainer *four times*," Kenzie said. "That's like a whole orthodontist." And she walked off down the hallway, sequins catching the light.

* * *

I checked everything that night in my room with the door locked. Every photo. Every time. Des had been right. Everybody's phone agreed with everybody else's phone. Colby's agreed with all of them. The two photos he'd captioned 11:15 and 11:52 had been taken at 12:21 and 12:38.

There were no photos of Theo between 10:47 and 12:21. None. Fifteen different people taking pictures of a basement, and not one of them caught him in that window.

I called Reyes, because some things need a voice.

She listened without interrupting. When I was done, she said, "The girl. Kenzie. She'll talk to me?"

"She said yes."

"With a parent."

"I'll tell her."

"Send me the files. All of them, not just the two. Send them from your phone directly to mine, and don't touch them before you do. Don't open them in some app, don't edit, don't crop." A pause. "And Wren. How did you get these?"

"Kenzie sent them to me. From the album. She was at the party. She's in the server."

"Nobody hacked anything. Nobody made a fake account."

"Not this time."

"Good," Reyes said. "*This* I can use."

* * *

She talked to Theo on Monday.

Voluntarily. That was the word she kept using, carefully, when she called me after. A voluntary conversation, at his house, with his mom present, just a few questions about some photo timestamps.

"It lasted about ninety seconds," she said. Her voice had the flat, careful sound of someone who'd already been angry about something once, in private, and didn't have the energy to be angry about it twice. "I asked him if he'd ever left Colby Reed's house Monday night. His mom said *of course not*. He didn't say anything. Then his dad came home, and his dad called somebody, and by the time I'd finished my coffee there was a lawyer in the living room."

"Already?"

"Theo's dad has a cousin who does contract work for the county. Knows exactly how these conversations are supposed to go." She breathed out. "The lawyer's position, more or less: his client has cooperated fully, the department has already closed this matter, and any further contact will be considered harassment of a minor. Which, technically, if I keep pushing without the department behind me, it kind of would be."

"So he just walks."

"For now, he walks." She was quiet. "I'm sorry, Wren. It's real work. It's the first real thing anybody's handed me on this that isn't a feeling. It's just not enough to move a family with a lawyer and a sheriff who doesn't want to be moved."

"He lied about where he was. He has a fake alibi."

"He has a friend who wrote a misleading caption on a party photo. That's not a crime. And a kid leaving a party for forty minutes isn't a crime either." She paused. "But I'll tell you one thing I noticed, and then I'll tell you to forget I said it."

"Okay."

"That lawyer didn't care at all about the photos. He barely looked at them. The only thing he wanted to make sure of, the one thing he kept steering his client away from, was 11:40." Reyes's voice went quiet. "Innocent kids' lawyers usually fight hardest about the thing that makes their client look bad. This one fought hardest about one specific minute."

I sat with that after we hung up. A boy on the side of the road for forty seconds. A lawyer guarding that minute like it was the only one that mattered.

Whatever had happened at 11:40, it was the thing Theo couldn't afford anyone to know.

* * *

The T number went the same way, only faster.

It was Priya's idea, and it was a good one. Camille's phone was on a family plan, the same as almost everybody's. And the account holder could log in online and see every number a phone on the plan had texted, and every number that had texted it, with times. Not the messages. Just the numbers and the times. It's the kind of thing parents use to check whether you were texting after midnight.

"I asked my aunt," Priya told me on Monday afternoon. We were in the library, at a table in the back corner, the one under the window that leaks. She'd started meeting me there after school without either of us ever saying we were going to. "I didn't know how to ask her. I just said I want to know who Camille was texting that night. And she looked at me for a really long time, and she got her laptop, and she logged in, and then she handed it to me and went in the other room. She said *I can't look. You look.*"

She turned her laptop around. She'd copied it into a document, neat, the way Priya does everything. She'd blacked out the last four digits of every number except one.

[[LOG]]
CAMILLE — MONDAY NIGHT (from carrier account, msgs only, no content)
10:58 PM — OUT — (503) ███-4417 ["T"]
11:01 PM — OUT — Theo (cell)
11:03 PM — IN — (503) ███-4417 ["T"]
11:12 PM — IN — Theo (cell)
11:20 PM — OUT — (503) ███-4417 ["T"]
11:24 PM — IN — (503) ███-4417 ["T"]
11:36 PM — IN — Theo (cell)
11:47 PM — OUT — (503) ███-4417 ["T"]
— nothing after 11:47 PM —

Also: no texts to or from ███-4417 on this account BEFORE 10:58 PM Monday. Ever.
[[/LOG]]

I read it twice. Then a third time, slower.

"She texted T first," I said. "At 10:58. And then three minutes later she texted Theo."

"And T answered her. Twice." Priya's finger moved down the column. "And Theo texted her twice. The second time at 11:36. That's right before—"

"Right before the road," I said. "Four minutes before the camera."

Priya looked up. "What camera?"

I hadn't told her about the camera.

I'd told her about the archive and the jacket, because she'd been there. I hadn't told her about the Fuel Stop, or Theo's limp, or the car at 4:58 in the morning. I'd told Reyes. I'd told Des. I'd kept Priya on a need-to-know basis without ever deciding to, the way I keep everybody.

I told her now. She listened with her face getting stiller and stiller.

"Okay," she said when I was done. Just that. Then she went back to her laptop.

"And the number?" I asked. "The T number."

"I did everything. Reverse lookups. Searched it on every platform. Searched it with and without the area code, with dashes, without. Checked if it was ever attached to a Venmo or a Cash App." She shook her head. "It's prepaid. The kind you buy at a gas station with a card. No name, no address. I called it this morning. It's disconnected." She said the next part with real frustration, like the number had personally insulted her. "Whoever had it bought it, used it, and threw it away. And it's not the same number that texted you, before you ask. Yours was an app. This was a real phone. Just a cheap one."

"So T's gone."

"T's gone. Whoever it was knew enough to disappear the second they weren't needed anymore." She stared at the screen. "That's either an adult who's done this before, or a kid who's more careful than any kid I've ever met. And I don't love either of those."

"No," I said.

* * *

It should have felt like a partnership, hitting a wall together. It did, for about ten minutes.

Then I said the wrong thing.

I didn't mean to say it the way I said it. I want that on the record, for whatever it's worth. We were sitting at the leaky table with the dead-end report between us, and three weeks of frustration finally cracked something open in my chest that I hadn't planned on opening in front of her.

"I think whoever's covering for Theo is the same kind of person who covered for whatever happened to my sister," I said. "I think that's why none of this ever breaks clean. This town protects the same people over and over. And I think Camille found a thread that went back to one of them, back to that summer, and I think that's why she's dead."

Priya went very still.

"Say that again," she said.

Her voice had a shape I hadn't heard before. Colder than the vigil. Colder than the hallway.

"I think Camille's case and Rowan's case are the same kind of case. Same kind of cover-up, maybe even the same people—"

"You've turned my cousin into a chapter in your sister's story."

She said it quietly. That was worse than if she'd yelled.

"I was listening to you just now," she said. "And for about four seconds I believed you actually cared about proving what happened to Camille. And then you said *my sister*, like it was the whole point. Like Camille was just a door you needed to open to get to the case you actually care about."

"That's not what I meant."

"Isn't it?" She leaned forward. "Be honest with me for one second, Wren. Can you do that? If Camille's death turned out to have nothing to do with Rowan. If the jacket was a coincidence. If T was just some random guy. If none of it connected back to your family. Would you still be sitting here every day working on this? Or would you have handed it to Reyes two weeks ago and gone back to finding people's retainers?"

I didn't answer fast enough.

That was the whole problem. I knew it in the silence, and so did she. The honest answer lived somewhere in the gap between who I wanted to be and who I actually was, and I hadn't closed that gap for myself yet, let alone for her.

"That's what I thought," she said. She started packing up her bag. Her hands were moving fast, angry-efficient. "You didn't tell me about the camera."

"I was going to—"

"You didn't tell me about the camera. You didn't tell me about the car at five in the morning. You didn't tell me Theo lied about where he was. Do you know what you told me? At the funeral, by her *shoes*, you told me you'd tell me what you were thinking in one day. *One day*, Wren. That was nine days ago." Her voice cracked, and this time she didn't stop it. "My cousin is dead. She's not a clue. She's not a stepping stone. She was a real person who fought with me over a group project two months before she died, and I was petty about it, and I never got to say sorry. And I have spent every single day since then trying to find one honest thing to say about who she actually was. And you've been standing next to me this whole time using her as a flashlight to look around your own house."

"I care about Camille. That's not fake."

"Maybe not." She zipped her bag. "But it's not first. And I needed it to be first. I needed one person in this whole thing whose top priority was actually her. And it was never going to be you. You've had a bigger case running in the background this whole time. And I was just a source. A useful cousin with access to the family phone bill."

She stood up. She stood there for a second like she was deciding whether there was anything else worth saying.

There wasn't, apparently.

She just looked at me. Not angry anymore, exactly. More like something had gone out. Some version of hope she'd been quietly keeping since the archive, that this could turn into something honest between us.

She walked out of the library.

* * *

I sat at the leaky table for a long time after she left. The library felt bigger than it had ten minutes earlier, and a lot emptier.

Ms. Feiler came by at some point with her cart of books to reshelve. She didn't say anything. She left a cough drop on the table next to my hand, the honey kind, and kept going.

Here's where I was, if I'm being as honest as Priya had just made me be.

Theo had an alibi that was provably fake, and a lawyer who made him untouchable. The one number that might have told me who Camille went up there to meet had scrubbed itself out of existence on purpose. Des had a real, reasonable fear keeping him away from the one place I needed him. And the person who'd spent three weeks cautiously deciding to trust me had just told me, correctly, that I hadn't deserved it.

I walked home without texting anybody. For me, that's rare enough to notice.

My dad's truck was in the driveway. The photo of Rowan was in its place at the bottom of the stairs, dusted, straight, the way it always is. And for the first time I could remember, I looked at it and didn't feel the regular ache of missing her.

I felt scared.

Scared that Priya was right. That under all the good reasons I had for doing this, there was a smaller, uglier one sitting at the bottom. That I'd been using people. Des, Priya, Kenzie, Delgado, Reyes. Using them the way you use a ladder, and then stepping off at the top without looking back down.

There's a person in my life who did that once. Who used up a house and a husband and a four-year-old, and then took the good suitcase and stepped off without looking down.

I've spent twelve years being sure I'm not her.

I opened the case log that night anyway. Not writing it down felt like its own kind of lie, and I'd done enough of those for one week.

# THE WRONG KIND OF MISSING

*Quiet Hollow Mystery — Book One*

J. Lowery

# Chapter One

Lighthouses get all the credit for saving people. Nobody talks about their other job, which is marking exactly where the rocks start.

At 6:52 on a Tuesday morning in October, I was about seventeen feet from that spot, crouched in wet grass, with my phone flashlight pointed at a shoe.

One shoe. The left one. White, clean, the expensive kind with the little stitched logo on the heel. It was sitting upright in the grass, laces still tied, like somebody had stepped out of it and meant to come right back.

That's not what shoes do when the person wearing them is at the bottom of a cliff.

* * *

My phone had started going off at 6:40, about a mile into my run.

The juniors server is forty-one people, one channel for memes, one for homework answers nobody admits to copying, and a map where everybody's location has been visible to everybody else since sixth grade. Nobody thinks about the map. It's like knowing what everyone's backpack looks like. It's just there.

Except at 6:40 somebody had thought about it.

kenz.ochoa: why is camille at the lighthouse rn

kenz.ochoa: like her dot has been there since last night??

jaydenw: phone died probably

kenz.ochoa: a dead phone doesn't stay ON the map tho

jaydenw: ok detective 💀

mia.k: she's prob asleep at someone's house and it's glitching

kenz.ochoa: the lighthouse isn't a house

Somebody posted a GIF of a cat falling off a table. The conversation moved on.

I stopped in the middle of the road and opened the map. Camille Malhotra's dot was sitting at the very top of Hollow Point, and according to the little gray timestamp under her name, it had been sitting there since 11:52 the night before.

Seven hours. Forty-one people could see that, and the only reaction had been a skull emoji.

I run the point most mornings before school. My dad thinks it's a healthy habit. I think it's the one hour of the day when nobody in my house is being careful around me, so, you know. We both get something out of it.

I was already heading that way. Then I saw the lights through the fog, red and blue smeared across the wet rock, and I did the one thing my dad has been asking me not to do since I was ten years old.

I ran toward it instead of away.

* * *

Here's how this town handles something bad, before it's even officially bad.

First there's whoever finds it. This time it was Gary Ostrander, out with his dog at first light, a story I'd end up hearing secondhand four separate times before lunch. Then there's whichever deputy gets the call. Today that was Deputy Brennan, who has known me since I was ten and still says my name like it's a warning label. She was stringing yellow tape between two fence posts and a trash can, making a square around the top of the stairs that was way too small, because nobody in Quiet Hollow has ever needed the tape to be very big.

And then, if you're lucky or unlucky depending on who you ask, there's whoever is already awake and running in that direction.

This morning, that was me.

The shoe was about four feet outside the tape.

I crouched next to it without touching it. The grass around it was flattened in a rough half-circle, the way grass gets when someone's been standing in one spot for a while, shifting their weight. Or two someones. I couldn't tell. The fog made everything look the same color.

"Wren." Brennan had spotted me. "Nope. Go home."

"Your tape's too small," I said. "There's a shoe out here."

She looked at the shoe. She looked at her tape. She said a word I'm not supposed to repeat and got on her radio.

That's how I ended up standing on the first landing of the old wooden stairs that go down the cliff, which is technically still outside the tape if you're generous about it, watching Detective Reyes work the rocks below.

Reyes is new. New for Quiet Hollow, anyway, which means she's been here a little over a year and people still ask her where she's *really* from. She was crouched near the bottom with her back to the water, and she didn't turn around when she said, "You're not supposed to be here."

"Neither is that shoe," I said. "It was outside the tape. It's a left. Where's the right one?"

She didn't answer.

That's usually how I find out I'm right about something.

* * *

I didn't know yet that it was Camille. Not for sure.

From the landing it was just a girl on the rocks below the old walkway, the part above the tide line where the railing has been rusted through since before I was born. Every parent in town has complained about that railing at every town meeting for ten years. Nobody has ever fixed it. There's always something more important to fund, like a new sign that says WELCOME TO QUIET HOLLOW in a nicer font.

Dark hair. A jacket bunched up wrong, the way clothes get when nobody's arranged you on purpose.

I didn't look at the rest. I want to be clear about that. People say a lot of things about me, and some of them are true, but I don't stare at the parts I don't need to see. I looked at the edges. The rocks. The tide coming in. The shoe up top, alone in the grass.

By 7:00, the story was three versions deep in every server the school has. Somebody's sister works dispatch and can't keep her mouth shut. Somebody's mom walks this stretch every morning at six and had already taken a photo she had no business taking. Everybody had a theory.

Nobody had the other shoe.

Then the coroner's van came crunching up the gravel, loud enough that the little crowd behind the tape finally went quiet, and a woman in a county windbreaker said the name out loud to Brennan, and it was official.

Camille Malhotra. Junior. Relay team. Sat two rows in front of me in chemistry and always turned around to borrow a pencil she never gave back.

And the version already moving faster than the ambulance was this: she slipped. Wet rocks, bad railing, ten years of nobody paying to fix it. Sad, but nobody's fault. The kind of accident this town keeps a folder for and hates opening.

Here's what I knew that nobody else in that crowd did.

Camille hated this lighthouse.

She told me so once, freshman year, when our biology class came up here to look at tide pools. She'd stayed on the bus. When I asked why, she said it in the flat voice people use when they're not even a little bit joking.

*My mom says a girl went off those rocks once and it wasn't really an accident. I'm never going up there.*

She meant Rowan.

Everybody in Quiet Hollow eventually means Rowan, whether they say her name or not.

* * *

I didn't say any of that out loud. You learn pretty fast not to, once you've watched enough adults do the flinch when my sister comes up. So I stood on the landing with my arms crossed against the cold and did the thing I'm apparently good at, the thing that's earned me a reputation that swings between *useful* and *unhinged* depending on which parent you ask and what day it is.

I looked for what didn't add up.

The shoe didn't add up. Camille double-checked locks. She reread her texts before she sent them. She was the only person I knew who actually used the little bookmark ribbon in library books. A girl like that doesn't just lose a shoe on a walk and keep going. Something happened between the walking and the falling, and *something happened* is a very different sentence from *she slipped*.

Then two EMTs came up the stairs with the stretcher, and I had to flatten myself against the rail of the landing to let them pass.

That's when the jacket happened.

It was navy. Workwear cut, heavy canvas, faded pale at the shoulders the way fabric only fades after years, not months. Too small for her, actually, now that I was close enough to see it properly. It had ridden up over her hip when they shifted her, and for maybe one second, before one of the EMTs realized I was standing there and turned his body to block me, I saw the lining.

Red and black check. That old-fashioned buffalo plaid.

The wind came up off the water right then, and it carried the smell of the jacket past me. Salt, mostly. And under the salt, something dry and woody, like the inside of an old dresser drawer.

Then they were past me and gone up the stairs, and I was holding the rail so tight my fingers hurt.

Because I knew that lining.

There's a photo of it in a shoebox in my closet. My sister, at a bonfire, laughing, wearing that exact jacket with the collar flipped up. It was taken six years and two months ago, the last week anybody in this town saw her alive.

* * *

I stayed on the landing longer than I should have. Long enough to hear something I wasn't supposed to.

Reyes had come up the stairs behind the stretcher and stopped at the top, near the tape, phone pressed to her ear, her back to me. I don't think she knew how well sound carries on those stairs. The cliff sort of cups it.

"No, it's on her. Locked." A pause. "Last outgoing is 11:47. A text. The contact's saved as a single letter. T." Another pause. "No last name. No, I don't know who that is, that's why I'm calling you." A longer pause. "ME's guessing somewhere between eleven and one. Yeah. I know what Boone's going to say. I'm asking you anyway."

11:47. And Camille's dot had frozen at 11:52.

Five minutes.

I did the math on that twice, the way you check a lock you already know is locked.

"Wren."

Reyes had turned around. She was looking at me with the expression adults use when they haven't decided yet whether you're a witness, a suspect, or a headache. With me it's usually all three before breakfast.

"This isn't a missing bike," she said.

"I know what it isn't."

"Go to school."

"It's 7:04. School doesn't start for fifty-six minutes."

She looked at me for about two seconds too long. Then she turned back to the scene, which was as close to permission as I was going to get out of her.

I didn't touch anything. Not the shoe, not the rail, nothing. I stood exactly where a person could stand if anybody ever asked, and I did what I've been doing since I was ten and found the Petersons' cat wedged behind their water heater after three grown adults had officially declared the cat gone.

I paid attention.

That's the whole trick. Nobody wants to hear that, because it sounds too easy. But it's true.

* * *

I walked home at 7:16 with my running shoes soaked through, sorting things into folders in my head.

*Accident, officially.* Wet rocks, bad railing, a decade of complaints nobody paid for. Everybody in town would believe it by lunch, because believing it is a lot less work than the alternative, and this town is extremely good at picking the version that's less work.

I've never been good at that. My dad would agree, if he ever talked about it directly instead of getting that tight look around his eyes whenever I bring it up.

He was in the kitchen when I came in, holding a mug of coffee like he'd forgotten it was there. "You heard about the accident up at the point?"

"Yeah," I said.

"Terrible." He looked at my wet shoes and then away from them. "You should eat something."

That was the entire conversation. That's how it goes in my house. We don't dig. Digging is a family activity we retired six years ago, at least as far as he's concerned.

I went upstairs, closed my door, and opened the other app. The one that isn't really a notebook. The one only Des knows I use.

I started typing before I'd even gotten my shoes off.

# Chapter Two

By the time I locked my bike at school, my brain had put the jacket in a folder labeled DEAL WITH THIS LATER.

Mostly, it worked.

Mostly.

"Wren. Wren. You're doing the thing."

Des fell into step beside me at the bike racks, backpack half-zipped, black hair sticking straight up in the back the way it does when he's slept even less than usual. He's been taller than me since seventh grade and has never once figured out what to do with the extra arm length. He pushed his glasses up with one knuckle and squinted at me.

"The staring-at-nothing thing," he said. "What'd you find?"

"A body."

"Okay, besides the body. You've got a body-*adjacent* face on."

This is the problem with having the same best friend since third grade. There's no version of you they haven't already catalogued. Des has a whole filing system for my faces. The bored one. The bored-but-pretending one. The one that means I've decided to do something my dad's going to hate. He reads them faster than I can control them, which used to bother me and now mostly just annoys me, because it's efficient, and I hate that it's efficient.

"I'm fine," I said. That sentence has never once ended a conversation with Des.

"Cool, love that for you." He didn't even slow down. "Anyway. Kenzie Ochoa's retainer is missing again. She's offering twelve dollars. I told her you don't do retainers anymore, but she says it's an emergency because picture day is Thursday and her teeth quote-unquote need to be seen."

"Her teeth are fine."

"That's between you and Kenzie."

This is a thing that happens to me now. Ever since the Petersons' cat, people bring me their small disasters the way other towns have a guy who fixes lawnmowers. Missing retainers. A bike somebody's little brother *definitely* didn't take. A dog that got out. A phone left on the bus.

What I don't do is cheating boyfriends. People ask. I say no. Not because I have some big moral code about it, but because cheating is never actually a mystery. It's just sad. And I don't like being handed sad things dressed up as puzzles.

I do lost things. Things with an actual answer at the end, if you're willing to look long enough.

Camille Malhotra didn't have an answer at the end. Camille Malhotra had a coroner's van and one shoe.

"Twelve is insulting," I told Des. "Tell her fifteen, or she can ask her mom to buy a new retainer. Which, fun fact, costs less than fifteen dollars. So really I'm doing her a favor by pricing myself out."

"I'll tell her you're booked."

"Tell her I'm booked."

* * *

We made it about four steps inside before I felt it.

There's a specific thing a hallway does when everyone already knows something and is trying to act like they don't. It gets louder, somehow. Half the school had a phone out. A girl by the trophy case was crying in the way that meant she'd talked to Camille exactly twice, both times about a group project, and was now in the front row of her grief anyway. Two sophomores were zooming in on somebody's photo of the cliff, arguing about whether you could see "her" in it.

You couldn't. I'd been there. You couldn't.

"You heard, right," Des said. It wasn't a question. His voice had dropped into the register he only uses when he's decided a bit isn't appropriate anymore. That doesn't happen often, so I always notice.

"I was there."

He stopped walking. "*There* there?"

"I run the point in the mornings, Des."

"I know you run the point. I didn't know you ran into an entire crime scene."

He said *crime scene* too loud. Three people turned around. He had the decency to look embarrassed and pulled me over by the water fountain.

"Are you okay?" he said, lower.

Nobody had asked me that yet. Not Reyes. Not Brennan. Definitely not my dad.

"I'm fine," I said again. It landed even worse the second time. Des just looked at me for a second, filing it under whatever face I was making, and didn't push.

That's the other thing about Des. He knows exactly which parts of me not to touch. It might be his most useful skill, more useful than the drone or the editing software or the fact that he can tell you what lunch period anyone in this school has off the top of his head. He knows where the walls are. He's just never once, in eight years, tried to find out what's behind them.

Either he doesn't want to know, or he already knows and he's being polite about it. I've never asked which.

"Okay," he said. "If you're fine, then I'm going to tell you something, and you're going to be fine about it."

"What."

"There's a photo going around. From this morning. Somebody's mom took it from the parking lot." He hesitated. "You're in it."

He showed me. It was grainy and zoomed in too far, but there I was, a small dark shape in running clothes on the cliff stairs, arms crossed, staring down at the rocks while everyone else stood behind the tape.

Somebody had already added a caption. *wren ashby said it's giving homicide* 💀

"I didn't say that," I said.

"I know you didn't say that."

"I don't even talk like that."

"Nobody talks like that, Wren. It's a caption. Captions don't have to be real." He took the phone back. "I'm just saying. People are looking at you today. Maybe don't do anything that makes them look harder."

* * *

The rest of the day went the way these days go.

A staff email went out during fourth period calling it "a tragic accident" and reminding everyone that grief counselors would be available in the library. Somehow that felt worse than no email at all, like the tragedy had been scheduled into a room with bad lighting between second and third lunch.

Ms. Feiler runs the library. Technically she counts as one of the counselors, even though her real job is the archive, the back room full of old yearbooks and microfilm and the town newspaper going back to when it was printed on basically parchment. She caught my eye across the room while the announcement played. She didn't say anything.

From Ms. Feiler, that's basically a hug.

Priya Malhotra wasn't at school. I noticed the way you notice an empty chair when you know exactly whose it is. I knew her a little. Everybody did. She runs *The Hollow Beacon*, the school news blog, the one that actually breaks stories sometimes instead of reposting the lunch menu. People are careful around Priya. She has a way of looking at you like she's already fact-checking your sentence before you've finished it.

I didn't know yet that she was Camille's cousin. I found that out the way everybody found out everything that day. A comment under a post. Then three different people telling me, separately, each one acting like they were the first.

* * *

I got home a little after four. My dad's truck was already in the driveway, which meant he'd left work early. He does that maybe twice a year, and he never explains why.

He was standing in the kitchen, not cooking anything. Just standing there with his hand flat on the counter, like he was checking whether it was still solid.

There was a casserole dish on the stove. Glass, covered in foil, with a little card tucked under the edge.

"Where'd that come from?" I asked.

"Nora Petrakis dropped it off." He said it like it was normal, and I guess in this town it is. Something happens, casseroles appear. "She heard you were up at the point this morning. Wanted to check on you."

I had to think for a second. Nora. Nora from the *Saturdays*. When I was little, before everything, my dad worked double shifts at the mill on weekends, and Nora used to come over and watch me and Rowan. She made grilled cheese cut into triangles and let us stay up for the end of whatever movie was on. She'd stopped coming the summer Rowan disappeared, which a lot of people did. I hadn't really thought about her in years.

"How did she even hear that?"

"How does anybody hear anything around here." He shrugged. "She sat with me for a while. Had a coffee. Asked how you were doing. If you'd seen anything upsetting up there." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I told her you're tough. That you're fine."

"I am fine."

He nodded, like that was a real answer.

I pulled the card out from under the foil. *Thinking of you both. Call if you need anything at all. — N.* Nice handwriting. The loopy, careful kind grown-ups had before phones.

"You eat?" Dad asked. That's what he says instead of most things.

"I'm not hungry."

He nodded again and went back to standing at the counter.

Behind him, in the front hall at the bottom of the stairs, there's a framed photo of Rowan at fifteen. She's laughing at something off-camera, wearing a green dress she wore exactly once, because she said it made her feel like someone else's daughter. She hated that dress. She kept it anyway, because she said it looked good on her even if it didn't feel good, and she wasn't about to waste a dress that looked that good just because of a feeling.

That was Rowan. She'd argue with you about a dress for twenty minutes and then wear it to prove a point nobody else was making.

Nobody's touched that photo in six years except to dust it, which my dad does himself, quietly, on a schedule I've never caught him at but can track anyway. The glass is always clean. The frame is always straight.

Her birthday's in April. Nobody in this house has said the word *birthday* out loud near that date since the year it happened. So April is either the quietest month in the Ashby house or the loudest, depending on how you measure quiet.

I looked at that photo longer than I meant to.

Then I went upstairs. I didn't say anything about the jacket. My dad didn't ask what was actually wrong. That's how we do things. It's worked fine for six years, mostly.

Right up until the exact week it stopped.

* * *

My phone buzzed a little after nine. A screenshot, forwarded through three different people before it reached me, of a post from the county news page.

SHERIFF'S DEPT: HOLLOW POINT DEATH "APPEARS ACCIDENTAL." NO SIGNS OF FOUL PLAY.

Under it, a quote from Sheriff Boone Lockhart about wet conditions and the dangers of the walkway after dark, and a reminder that the path is closed from sunset to sunrise.

Twelve hours. It had taken them twelve hours to decide nothing happened.

I read it twice. Then I opened the app I don't call a notebook and typed one line before I turned off the light.

# Chapter Three

The word *accidental* made it into Wednesday's morning announcements.

This is a school that once read the football scores over the intercom the same week a kid's grandfather died, so the bar was already low. Somehow we still found a way under it.

Principal Halloran's voice came through the speaker tinny and careful. The sheriff's department had made a preliminary finding. The family had asked for privacy. There would be a candlelight gathering at Hollow Point that evening at seven. Please be respectful of the Malhotra family during this difficult time.

Nobody in first period was being respectful. They were doing what people do instead, which is talk about being respectful very loudly.

"Accidental," I said, mostly to myself.

Des heard it from the next desk. "You say that like it's a made-up word."

"It might as well be. It's not a finding. It's a shrug with a badge on it."

"Okay, Sherlock." He said it without any edge. It was Des for *keep it down, people are sad two feet away from you*. He wasn't wrong. I stopped talking.

I didn't stop thinking it.

Here's the thing about *accidental* that nobody at the sheriff's department loses sleep over. It doesn't describe what happened. It describes what nobody's going to bother proving didn't happen. Wet rocks and a bad railing get you *accidental* every time, because an accident doesn't need a person. And a person is the expensive part of any investigation. A person needs a warrant, a lawyer, a reason a judge will sign off on.

A missing shoe doesn't need any of that. A missing shoe just needs somebody to stop looking for it.

I hadn't stopped. Stopping has never really been a setting I have.

* * *

The vigil was smaller than I expected, and somehow worse because of it.

Somebody's older sister had ordered two hundred electric candles online because real ones felt like a fire hazard on a cliff. Which, fair. But it meant Hollow Point was lit up in that flat blue-white LED glow that makes everyone look a little bit like a ghost already. The lighthouse beam swept over us every eleven seconds. I counted without meaning to.

A teacher I didn't recognize was crying harder than anyone who'd actually known Camille. A group of freshmen stood near the back filming for their stories. I understand why people do that. I also find it kind of unbearable. You can understand something completely and still not want to watch it happen.

The railing where she went over had been wrapped in fresh yellow tape. Somebody had tied a bunch of grocery-store sunflowers to it anyway.

I saw Priya near the tape, apart from everyone else, arms crossed against the wind coming off the water. She's shorter than me, with long black hair she usually wears in a sleek ponytail that never has a single strand out of place. Tonight it was down and blowing across her face, and she wasn't fixing it, and somehow that told me more than anything else about how she was doing.

I walked over. I don't know what I thought I was going to say. Something like *I'm sorry*. Something like *I saw things yesterday that don't add up, and you might be the only person here who'd actually want to know*.

I got about four words in.

"Don't," Priya said. She didn't look at me. "I know what you do. I know what people say you do. My cousin is not going to be your little project."

"That's not—"

"I saw the photo." Now she did look at me, and that was worse. "Somebody's mom sent it to me. You, standing on the stairs, doing your detective face, before they'd even moved her. She's not even buried yet, Wren."

There isn't a good response to that. I tried one anyway. "I think something's wrong with the ruling."

"Everybody thinks something's wrong with the ruling. That's what grief feels like. It doesn't mean you get to turn it into your thing." She said *thing* like it was a word she'd been saving up. "You've got a whole reputation built on other people's disasters. I'm not going to be the next chapter."

I didn't have anything for that. That almost never happens to me.

I stood there in the cold blue light with my hands in my pockets and let her walk away. She went back to her aunt, Camille's mom, who was standing near the lighthouse door looking like someone had unplugged her. And next to her aunt, holding her up with one arm around her waist, was a woman I recognized a beat later than I should have.

Nora Petrakis.

She'd gotten older in the way adults do when you stop seeing them for a while, all at once instead of gradually. Gray streaks in her hair that hadn't been there before. Reading glasses pushed up on her head. But it was her. Same soft cardigan kind of person. Same way of standing a little too close to whoever she was comforting, like closeness was a thing she could give them.

Priya folded into the two of them. The three of them held onto each other in the way people do when they're the only ones in a crowd actually losing something, instead of just watching it happen to someone else.

I looked away, because it felt like the kind of thing you should look away from.

* * *

That's when Theo Marsh showed up.

He came up the gravel path from the parking lot with three of his friends around him like a security detail. Colby Reed, who runs half the parties in this town. Two guys from the soccer team I only knew by their jersey numbers. Theo was in the middle, and he was limping. He'd torn a ligament in September and spent the last month hobbling around the halls in a knee brace, complaining to anyone who'd hold still about losing the last three games of his senior season.

He didn't go to Camille's family.

That's the part I noticed. He was her boyfriend. They'd been together since last spring. Every couple in school who'd ever posted a hard launch had tagged them as goals at least once. And he stopped at the back of the crowd with his friends and stayed there, hood up, hands jammed in his pockets, looking at the lighthouse and not at anybody.

Across the crowd, Nora's head turned. Just for a second. Toward the back, where he was standing.

Then she turned back to Camille's mom and smoothed a hand down her back, and I told myself it was nothing. Everybody was looking at everybody. It was that kind of night.

* * *

I was heading for the parking lot when someone said my name.

"Wren? Wren Ashby?"

Nora had left Priya and her aunt with a cluster of other women and was coming toward me, arms already opening. Before I could decide how I felt about it, she'd pulled me into a hug.

She smelled like dryer sheets and something woody underneath, like the inside of an old dresser drawer. It was a nice smell. A grandma-house smell. For some reason it made the back of my neck prickle, the way a word does when it's on the tip of your tongue.

"Look at you," she said, holding me at arm's length. "The last time I saw you up close, you were missing both front teeth."

"Hi, Nora."

"Oh, honey." Her eyes were wet. She kept her hands on my arms. "I heard you were the one up here yesterday morning. Your dad said you were okay, but I just—" She shook her head. "That must have been awful. You didn't see her, did you? Up close?"

"Not really," I said. "Mostly the police."

Something in her shoulders let go a little. "Good. That's good. Some things you don't need living in your head."

She squeezed my hand. That's when I noticed the bandage. A big square one, across the palm of her right hand, the kind you put on when a regular Band-Aid won't cover it.

"What happened to your hand?"

"Oh." She laughed and looked down at it like she'd forgotten it was there. "Me versus a cheese grater. The cheese grater won." She let go of me and patted my cheek, the way she used to when I was seven. "You take care of yourself, okay? And your dad. He puts on a good face, but he's been through enough."

"Thanks for the casserole," I said.

"Anytime, honey. Anytime at all."

She went back to the family. I stood there a second longer than I needed to, with that woody smell still sort of hanging in the air, trying to figure out why it bothered me.

I couldn't. So I filed it under *things that are probably nothing*, which is a folder I've been trying to keep smaller lately.

* * *

Des found me a few minutes later at the edge of the gravel. He didn't ask what happened with Priya. He'd seen. He just stood next to me for a while, close enough that our shoulders almost touched.

From Des, that's a whole sentence.

"She's not wrong," he said eventually. "About the reputation thing."

"I know she's not wrong."

"Doesn't mean you're wrong either."

That's the most Des thing anyone has ever said to me. Both things true at once. No interest in picking a side. Just handing you the second half so you don't have to carry the first half by yourself.

"Theo didn't go talk to her family," I said.

Des followed my eyes to the back of the crowd. Theo and his friends were already leaving, Theo's limp making him fall a step behind the others on the gravel.

"Maybe he doesn't know what to say," Des said. "Guys like Theo are good at parties. They're not good at this."

"Maybe."

"You're making the face."

"I'm not making any face."

"You're making the *maybe* face. The one where maybe means no."

I didn't answer. The lighthouse beam swept over us. Eleven seconds. Then again.

* * *

My dad was still up when I got home, which almost never happens past nine. He was at the kitchen table with the county news page open on his laptop. The same headline everyone had been sharing all day. *APPEARS ACCIDENTAL*, in that bold local-news font that makes everything look more official than it is.

"You went to the point," he said. Not a question. Somebody had told him, or he'd just known, the way he sometimes knows things about me without me saying them. That's either love or surveillance, and I've never figured out which.

"It was a vigil, Dad. Half the school went."

"That's not what I mean and you know it." He closed the laptop. For my dad, that's the same as standing up and raising his voice. "I know what you're doing. The face you get. I've seen that face before."

I knew exactly whose face he meant. So did he. Neither of us said her name. That's the deal we made without ever actually making it, the unspoken agreement that has kept this house standing, more or less, for six years.

"This isn't the same," I said.

"It's never the same. That doesn't mean it isn't the same thing happening again." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, the way he does when he's more tired than the hour can explain. "I can't do this twice, Wren. I mean that. I don't have it in me to watch you go looking for something in a case that isn't yours and come back different."

"I'm not looking for anything."

"You found a shoe before the police did."

I didn't have an answer for that either. That was two people who'd beaten me in an argument in one night. Under any other circumstances, it would have been a personal record.

"Let the department do their job," he said, quieter now. "Let this one go. Please."

I said okay.

I want that on the record. I actually said the word out loud. And I think I even meant it, for the six seconds it took to say it. Then I went upstairs and shut my door, and I stopped meaning it the second I sat down on my bed.

* * *

I didn't open the app. I just sat there in the dark, running the numbers the way I do when I'm trying to talk myself out of something I've already decided.

A shoe with no partner, sitting outside the tape.

A jacket that didn't fit her.

A text at 11:47 to someone saved as one letter, and a dot that stopped moving five minutes later.

A boyfriend who stood at the back of his girlfriend's vigil with his hood up.

And on the top shelf of my own closet, in a shoebox I hadn't opened in longer than I wanted to admit, a photo of a jacket I was now almost sure I'd seen twice. Once on my sister. And once on a dead girl who used to be scared of the exact place she died.

I didn't need to open the app. I already knew what I'd write.

I just wasn't ready to make it real by typing it.

Not yet.

Soon.

# Chapter Four

Camille's account was still up four days later. That's how I ended up thinking about Rowan's.

I wasn't looking for it. I was lying on my bed Saturday afternoon, scrolling the way everybody was scrolling that week, past Camille's profile picture with a hundred candle emojis under it. People who'd talked to her twice in their lives were writing whole paragraphs. Someone had made a slideshow set to a song Camille would have hated. Someone else had posted a photo of the lighthouse at sunset with the caption *fly high angel*, which, considering how she died, nobody should have let them post.

Then Kenzie Ochoa commented under the slideshow.

[[LOG]]
kenz.ochoa: is it weird that it's just still there
kenz.ochoa: like her account. it's just sitting there like she could post any second
[[/LOG]]

Nobody liked it. Nobody answered. I read it four times.

Kenzie is not a deep person. Kenzie loses her retainer roughly once a month and once tried to pay me in Starbucks stars. But Kenzie had been the only one in a server of forty-one people to notice that a dot wasn't moving, and now she'd noticed this, and she was right about it too.

Nobody deletes anything anymore. Nobody deactivates anything either. Not on purpose. Because closing an account means admitting the person isn't coming back to use it, and nobody wants to be the one who clicks that button. So accounts don't die. They just go quiet and sit there until a birthday reminder pops up and ruins somebody's whole week.

I knew that better than Kenzie did.

* * *

Rowan's account is public. It's always been public. She used to say there was no point taking a good photo if only nine people got to see it.

I hadn't looked at it in almost two years. That wasn't an accident. When I was twelve I muted it, and then I unmuted it, and then I muted it again, and then I deleted the app off my phone for a month and told Des it was a digital detox. He didn't believe me. He didn't say anything either.

I typed her handle into the search bar with my thumb and stopped before I hit enter.

It was 3:20 in the afternoon. My dad was at the mill until six. The house made the small settling noises it makes when it's empty, the fridge humming, the clock in the kitchen that runs four minutes fast because nobody's fixed it since Rowan set it that way so she'd stop being late. If I was going to do this, there would never be a better time, and that was exactly the problem. There's never a better time. There's just now, and then later, and then you're twelve and muting her again.

I hit enter.

The profile loaded slowly, like the app wasn't sure it should still be answering. And then there she was, in a grid of little squares.

My sister at seventeen.

Her hair was doing the thing it always did when it was humid, which in Quiet Hollow is always. She was laughing in about half the pictures. In most of them, her own arm was holding the camera, because Rowan didn't trust anyone else to get her good side. She used to say everyone else took photos of what she looked like, and she took photos of what she was like.

I told myself I was going to scroll like an investigator. Methodically. Top to bottom. Notes.

I didn't. I sat there for twenty minutes just looking. Rowan on the hood of somebody's car with a slushie. Rowan in a cap and gown at a friend's graduation, a year before her own that she never got to. A blurry picture of my sneakers on our front steps, with the caption *the gremlin wants to come to the beach. the answer is no.* I was nine in that picture. I'd forgotten it existed. I'd forgotten she ever called me the gremlin, and remembering it hit somewhere I don't usually let things hit.

There's a specific kind of quiet when you're looking at someone who doesn't know what's coming. Every caption was written by a girl who thought she had all the time in the world. That's the part nobody warns you about. Not the sad pictures. The happy ones.

Then I got to the top of the grid, which is the bottom of the story, and there it was.

Her last post.

* * *

It was a group shot at the bonfire beach. Maybe a dozen kids I half-recognized, all of them six years younger than they are now, lit orange from the fire. A couple of them have kids of their own these days. One of them sells real estate in Astoria and has her face on a bench by the ferry.

Rowan was front and center, holding the phone out at arm's length with her head tipped back mid-laugh.

She was wearing the jacket.

Navy canvas. Workwear cut. The collar flipped up the way she always wore collars, like she was in a movie about herself. The fire was behind her, and it lit up the edge of the open front, and there it was. Red and black check. That old-fashioned buffalo plaid.

I zoomed in until the picture broke apart into squares. It didn't tell me anything new. I'd already known since the EMTs carried Camille past me on the stairs and the wind brought me the smell of salt and old wood. This was just the part where I couldn't tell myself I might be wrong.

The caption said:

[[LOG]]
rowan.ash: last bonfire of summer 🔥 in my favorite hand-me-down. never giving this back lol
[[/LOG]]

Posted the second week of August, six years ago.

She never posted again.

I read the caption maybe six times. *Never giving this back.* It was a joke. That was obvious. She was being Rowan. But a joke like that still has to come from somewhere. You don't joke about not giving something back unless it belonged to somebody else first.

I scrolled the comments. Forty-three of them. Most were from that week. *🔥🔥🔥. ok model. who's driving after.* Then, starting about five days later, they changed.

*Rowan where are you.*

*Please call your dad.*

*Praying for you girl come home.*

There were comments from the next August, and the August after that. Fewer every year. The last one was from two years ago, from an account with no profile picture. Just one word: *still.*

I didn't know who'd written it. I clicked on the account. It was empty. No posts, no followers, nothing. Somebody had made it just to leave that one word under my sister's last picture and then never used it again.

I screenshotted it. I don't know why. It wasn't evidence of anything. It just felt like the kind of thing you should keep.

None of the comments mentioned the jacket. Nobody had tagged who it came from. Whoever had handed my sister her favorite hand-me-down wasn't in six years of a very online seventeen-year-old's account, which told me something, even if I didn't know what yet.

People don't usually stay untagged by accident.

* * *

I got up and went to my closet.

The shoebox lives on the top shelf, behind a stack of yearbooks nobody has opened since they were signed. I had to stand on my desk chair to reach it. I hadn't taken it down in almost two years either. I'd been keeping a lot of things at almost two years.

It's not a shrine. I want to be clear about that, since people in this town already think I'm a little much. It's a Nike box with a water stain on one side from the year a pipe leaked in the wall. I always put it back with the lid seam facing the wall so the stain is the last thing I see instead of the first. That sounds like a weird thing to care about. It is a weird thing to care about. When something's this fragile, you build little rules around it without ever really deciding to.

I sat on my bedroom floor and opened it.

One of Rowan's hair ties, the cloth kind, faded from purple to mostly gray. A ticket stub for a concert in Portland she went to without me, and came back from with a voice so wrecked from screaming that she had to write me notes for two days. A guitar pick from the summer she decided to learn guitar and quit after eleven days. A handful of printed photos.

And folded in quarters at the very bottom, a sheet of paper that had gone soft as cloth along the creases.

I knew what it was before I opened it. I'd put it there myself when I was eleven. It had come off the telephone pole at the corner of Fir and Chestnut after the rain had gotten to it, and I'd taken it home because I couldn't stand the idea of it rotting on a pole where people walked their dogs.

MISSING, it said across the top. Then Rowan's school picture, the one where she's trying not to smile and failing. Then the details, in the plain font my dad picked at the copy shop because he couldn't make himself choose anything else.

[[LOG]]
ROWAN ELISE ASHBY, 17
Height 5'6". Long brown hair. Brown eyes.
LAST SEEN: bonfire beach, Quiet Hollow, approx. 11:30 PM
LAST SEEN WEARING: navy canvas work jacket with red & black plaid lining, jean shorts, white canvas sneakers.
If you have ANY information please call Cal Ashby or the Quiet Hollow Sheriff's Department.
[[/LOG]]

I'd read that poster so many times when I was eleven that I could still recite it. I'd never thought about one specific line.

*Last seen wearing.*

They never found her. Everyone knows that. That's the whole story of my family, the one people in this town tell in a lowered voice at the grocery store. What I'd never let myself think about, until that afternoon on my bedroom floor, was that they never found anything she had on, either. Not a shoe. Not her phone. Not the jacket.

Whatever happened to Rowan happened to all of it.

So either the jacket on Camille was just a jacket that looked a lot like it, a coincidence, the kind of thing Des would tell me to put in the maybe folder.

Or it wasn't. And if it wasn't, then my sister's jacket had been somewhere for six years. Somebody had been keeping it.

I sat very still and let that thought finish arriving.

* * *

I should explain something about my family, because it matters, and because I'm not going to explain it again.

Rowan is my half-sister. Same dad. She was seven years older than me, which is enough of a gap that she was half a babysitter and half a sister, and she was bad at both and I worshipped her anyway.

My mom isn't in this story. That's not me being dramatic. It's just accurate. She left when I was four. She didn't vanish. She packed. She took the good suitcase and the car with the working heater, and she left a note on the counter that my dad has never shown me and I have never asked to see. Every few years a birthday card shows up with a postmark from a different state. My dad puts them on my desk without saying anything. I put them in a drawer without opening them.

So I know there are two kinds of missing. There's the kind where somebody chooses to go, and the people they leave have to live with that choice forever. And there's the other kind.

My mother is the first kind. I've had twelve years to get used to that, and mostly I have.

Rowan is the wrong kind.

Rowan didn't take the good suitcase. Rowan left her phone charger plugged in by her bed and a library book two weeks overdue and a half-finished bottle of the strawberry kombucha she was obsessed with that summer, which my dad didn't throw out until the following March. People who choose to leave don't leave their kombucha.

Some days I'm scared there's a version of me that could pack the good suitcase. That's the part I don't say to anybody. I think that's why I can't put things down. Lost cats, lost bikes, lost retainers. People who look back at everything are not the kind of people who leave. At least that's the theory I've been running since I was ten.

I've never asked Des if he thinks it holds up.

* * *

I took a picture of the poster, and a picture of the photo on Rowan's account, and put them side by side on my screen.

Same jacket. Same lining. Same flipped-up collar, because Rowan never wore a collar down in her life.

Then I put everything back in the shoebox the way it had been. The hair tie on top. The poster folded at the bottom. Lid seam to the wall. Stain facing in.

I climbed onto the chair and slid it back behind the yearbooks, exactly where it had been. I didn't know yet why it was going to matter that I'd done it so carefully.

Then I sat on my bed and did the math I'd been avoiding.

Camille hadn't been alive when Rowan was famous for being missing. Well, she had, but she'd been eleven, and she'd lived across town, and she'd been scared of the lighthouse because her mom told her a story. Camille Malhotra had no reason to have my sister's jacket. Camille Malhotra had probably never been within ten feet of my sister.

Somebody had, though.

Here's the honest part, since I'm being honest. I didn't decide to look into Camille's death because I believed in justice or because I owed her anything. I barely knew her. She borrowed my pencils. Somewhere between the lighthouse stairs and my bedroom floor, this had stopped being only Camille's case, and I've never once been able to walk away from something once it's mine. My dad would say that's not a healthy way to be a person. My dad might be right.

It didn't change anything.

* * *

I picked up my phone and texted Des before I could talk myself out of how big this was.

[[LOG]]
me: ok. I need your help with something and it's not a retainer
Des: oh no
Des: the tone of this text is doing a lot of work wren
me: can you come over
Des: it's saturday. I have a whole saturday planned
me: what's the plan
Des: there was going to be a nap
me: des
[[/LOG]]

The dots appeared and disappeared twice. From Des, that means he already knows his answer and is stalling on the part where he admits it.

[[LOG]]
Des: what did you find
me: come over and I'll show you
Des: that's what people say in horror movies right before
Des: give me twenty minutes
[[/LOG]]

I put the phone face down on my comforter and looked at the poster photo on my screen again. The plain black letters my dad picked out at the copy shop when he still thought posters worked.

*LAST SEEN WEARING: navy canvas work jacket with red & black plaid lining.*

Six years ago that jacket walked off a beach with my sister and disappeared.

Four days ago, I watched it come up the cliff stairs on somebody else.


# Chapter Five

Des showed up in nineteen minutes, which for Des is basically teleportation.

He came in through the garage, the way he has since we were nine. Our garage door sticks. You have to lift it about a foot, lean your weight on the left side until something in the track clunks, and then shove. Des learned the trick in fourth grade. Before that, the only people who knew it were me, my dad, Rowan, and whoever was watching us on Saturdays. My dad has been saying he'll fix it since before I could ride a bike.

I heard the clunk, then the shove, then Des in the kitchen saying, "I'm here, I'm starving, those are related."

He was already in the fridge when I came downstairs. He'd brought his laptop bag and a hoodie that said MATHLETES DO IT WITH CONFIDENCE INTERVALS, which his mom bought him as a joke and which he wears completely unironically.

"You don't have to feed me," he said, taking out a block of cheddar. "But I'm going to make quesadillas, and if you want one you have to say so now, because I'm not making a second round."

"I'm not hungry."

"So that's a yes." He found the grater in the drawer on the first try. Des knows where everything in our kitchen is. Sometimes I think he knows better than I do.

He grated cheese and I watched him and didn't say anything, and he let me not say anything, which is one of the main things Des is for. About halfway through the block he swore and stuck his hand in his mouth.

"Every time," he said around his knuckle. "Every single time. It's always the knuckles."

"Maybe stop grating your knuckles."

"That's not how graters work, Wren. The grater chooses." He ran his hand under the faucet, inspected the scrape, decided he'd live. "Okay. You've got the face. The one where you've decided something and you're waiting for me to catch up."

So I told him.

* * *

I told him all of it, sitting on the counter while he flipped tortillas in a pan. The shoe outside the tape. The 11:47 text to somebody saved as T. The dot that stopped at 11:52. The jacket on the stairs, the lining, the smell of salt and old wood. Rowan's last post. The poster at the bottom of my shoebox, and the line I'd somehow never read properly in six years.

*Last seen wearing.*

I told him about Priya at the vigil, and my dad at the kitchen table, and how they'd both been right about me and it hadn't changed anything.

Des didn't say anything for about ten seconds after I finished. For a person who fills silence for a living, ten seconds is a long time.

He slid a quesadilla onto a plate, cut it into triangles, and handed it to me. Then he leaned against the stove with his arms crossed.

"Okay," he said. "So. Just to make sure I understand what you're asking. You want to investigate a death the police already called an accident. Because you think it's connected to your sister. Because of a jacket."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It is a little insane, Wren."

"The jacket isn't a coincidence."

"I didn't say it was a coincidence. I said it was insane. Those are different." He pushed his glasses up with one knuckle, the scraped one, and winced. "I need you to actually hear this next part. Not the part where I say yes. The part before."

"Okay."

He looked at the floor for a second, which Des almost never does.

"I'm scared of you sometimes," he said. "Not of you. For you. For the version of you that shows up when something gets this close to Rowan. I remember sixth grade. I remember you telling the whole lunch table that Rowan moved to Seattle to do hair, like if you said it enough times it would turn into true. You had details, Wren. You had a salon name."

"Shear Madness."

"Shear Madness," he agreed. "I'm not saying that to be a jerk. I'm saying somebody in this conversation has to remember that, and it's apparently going to be me, because you've already decided you're doing this whether I help or not."

I had. He knew I had. That's the problem with Des. You can't even lie to him efficiently.

"I've decided," I said. "That part isn't up for debate. What's up for debate is whether I do it with somebody who actually knows how to pull a video file off a security system, or by myself with a flashlight and bad decisions."

"That's not a real choice."

"No. But I'm giving you the illusion of one, which is basically respect."

The corner of his mouth went first. That's how I always know. His face catches up a second later.

"Fine," he said. "But I'm the brakes. Not the steering wheel. The brakes. If I say we're done for the day, we're done for the day, and you don't get to make the face at me about it later."

"Deal."

"I mean it."

"I said deal."

He looked at me a while longer, like he was deciding whether a contract signed by me was legally binding. Then he went and got his laptop out of his bag, which with Des is the actual signature. The words are mostly decorative once the laptop comes out.

* * *

We built the board in the garage, because the garage has a whiteboard my dad bought for a fantasy football draft and then used exactly once. Des wrote across the top in green marker, the only one that still worked.

He stepped back, looked at it, and erased CAMILLE CASE and wrote THE WRONG KIND OF MISSING instead.

"What's that supposed to mean?" I said.

"You said it. In the kitchen, about your mom and Rowan. I thought it was good."

I hadn't realized I'd said it out loud. I didn't erase it.

By eight o'clock the whiteboard looked like this.

[[LOG]]

- Last text sent 11:47 PM to contact "T."

Des tapped the green marker against the T.

"This is the one that actually bothers me," he said. "Everybody in this school is in at least four servers with everybody else. Even the kids who don't talk to anyone share a server with somebody. One letter and no overlap means either a burner, or somebody being really careful. And regular seventeen-year-olds aren't that careful."

"Adults are."

He looked at me. "Yeah," he said. "They are."

Neither of us said anything else about it. We both just looked at the board for a second, at the shape the empty spaces were making.

"Also," Des said. "Can we talk about the 11:52 thing? Because I've been thinking about it since you said it, and it's weird."

"Weird how?"

He pulled out his phone and opened the map. Forty-one little faces on a little gray town. Mine was at home. His was at home too, because he'd shared his with mine years ago and never thought about it again.

"When somebody's phone dies," he said, "you see their last location, but it goes gray, and it says *last updated* with a time, and after a while it says *location unavailable*. Kenzie was right on Tuesday. A dead phone doesn't just sit there all bright like everything's normal."

"Camille's didn't go gray."

"Right. It stayed exactly where it was, looking normal, with the time under it. That's what it looks like when you pause your location. You hit the little ghost button, and everybody sees wherever you were when you hit it, frozen, until you turn it back on." He set his phone on the workbench. "Camille's phone was on her, and it was on. Reyes said so. You heard her. So it didn't die at 11:52. Somebody paused it."

"Or she did."

"Or she did. But why do you pause your location?"

I knew the answer. Everybody our age knows the answer. You pause your location when you're going somewhere you don't want anyone to know you went.

"She went up there to meet somebody," I said slowly. "And at 11:52, either she decided she didn't want anybody watching. Or somebody told her to turn it off."

Des wrote GHOST MODE 11:52 under KNOWN and put two question marks after it. Then he put a third.

* * *

My dad came home at a quarter past six, smelling like sawdust and machine oil the way he always does after a mill shift. He stopped in the doorway between the kitchen and the garage and looked at the two of us, and at the whiteboard, which I'd turned around to face the wall about two seconds after I heard his truck. Des was sitting on the old couch with his laptop, looking extremely innocent, which on Des looks extremely guilty.

"Desmond," my dad said.

"Mr. Ashby."

"You staying for dinner?"

"I already ate your cheese."

"That's not what I asked."

"Then yes, please, thank you."

My dad almost smiled. He likes Des. Des is one of the very few people my dad treats like part of the furniture, in a good way. Then his eyes went to the whiteboard, facing the wall, and stayed there just a little too long.

He didn't ask. He turned around and went inside and started banging pans in a way that meant spaghetti.

"He knows," Des said quietly.

"He doesn't know."

"He knows something. He did the thing with his jaw."

"He always does the thing with his jaw."

Des didn't push. But he looked at the door my dad had gone through for a while, and I could tell he was filing it.

* * *

"Okay," I said, after dinner, after my dad had gone to fall asleep in front of a college football game in the living room. "There's one camera on that whole stretch of road. The Fuel Stop, before you get to the point. If Camille walked out there Monday night, or somebody drove her, it caught something."

"You know Delgado isn't going to just hand security footage to a minor."

"He's going to hand it to the minor who found his generator in Terry Boskin's garage after Terry borrowed it for six months."

Des considered this. "Okay, that's fair. That man loves you."

"He tolerates me. It's different. But it works in our favor."

"So we go Monday after school."

I'd been doing math in my head since the moment Des said the word camera. "We can't wait till Monday."

"Why not?"

"Because I asked Delgado last spring how long his system keeps footage. When Kenzie's bike got stolen off the rack outside his store. He said seven days. Then it records over itself." I looked at Des. "Camille died Monday night. Monday night gets erased Monday night."

Des stared at me. "You remember the retention policy of a gas station's security camera from a bike case six months ago?"

"It was a nice bike."

"That's deeply unsettling," he said. Then he opened his phone and checked his calendar. "I'm at my grandma's for lunch tomorrow, it's her birthday, I can't get out of it unless one of us is actively dying. I can be back by four."

"Delgado closes at six on Sundays."

"Then four-thirty."

He closed the laptop, which meant it was as settled as it was going to get. He sat there a second longer, looking at the back of the whiteboard like he could see through it. Like he was trying to memorize the shape of the thing before it got bigger.

"For the record," he said, standing up and shouldering his bag, "this is the kind of thing that ends with one of us in real trouble."

"Probably."

"You're supposed to disagree with me there."

"I'm supposed to be honest with you there. And you like me because I pick honest."

He didn't have a comeback for that. He went out through the garage, and I heard the lift, the lean, the clunk, the shove, and then the door coming down behind him with the long complaining squeal it's made my whole life.

* * *

We got to the Fuel Stop on Sunday at 4:34.

That's four minutes later than I'd said and six minutes earlier than I actually ever get anywhere. Des knew this about me and had built the six minutes into his own plan without telling me. That's a level of managing me that I find both insulting and extremely useful.

The Fuel Stop is the last building on the road before it narrows into the curves that go out to the point. Two pumps, a store that sells bait and beef jerky and phone chargers that break in a week, and a single camera mounted on a pole at the edge of the lot, pointed at the pumps and, if you're lucky, about forty feet of road.

Delgado was behind the counter restocking lottery tickets. He looked up when the bell over the door jingled and didn't seem surprised to see me. In this town that's its own kind of compliment.

"You're not here about a generator," he said.

"I'm not here about a generator."

"Whatever it is, make it quick. Truck's coming at five."

I gave him the short version. A friend. Monday night. Just eyes on the road for a little while around midnight, nothing that would get him in trouble.

He didn't say anything for a second. He just looked at me, then at Des, then back at me. Then he put the stack of tickets down.

"Funny," he said. "That detective asked me the same thing."

My stomach did something. "Reyes?"

"The new one. Dark hair, looks like she hasn't slept since she moved here." He shrugged. "She came by Wednesday morning. Asked if the camera caught the road Monday night. I said maybe, depends on the angle. She said she'd send somebody for it." He shrugged again. "Called back an hour later. Said never mind. Said the department didn't need it after all."

"Did she say why?"

"Didn't ask. Not my business." He looked at me for a long moment. "But she didn't sound like it was her idea."

Des and I didn't look at each other. We didn't have to.

Delgado walked us back past the coolers to a little office the size of a closet, with a desk and a mini fridge and a monitor that looked older than both of us put together. He jiggled the mouse until it woke up and clicked through a program with a lot of gray boxes.

"Monday's still on here," he said. "Barely. It goes tomorrow." He pointed at Des. "You know how to work this?"

"I can figure it out."

"Twenty minutes," Delgado said. "Then I really do need my office back."

He left. Des sat down at the keyboard like he was settling into a cockpit, and I stood behind him with my arms crossed and my heart going faster than standing still should make it go.

He found Monday night. He dragged the little slider to 11:00 PM and hit play at four times speed.

The camera's view was black and white and grainy. The pumps in the foreground. The edge of the store's light. Then the road beyond, a strip of wet asphalt going off toward the point, and the dark where the light gave up.

Nothing happened. Nothing happened some more. The timestamp in the corner crawled forward. 11:10. 11:18. A moth went across the lens and both of us jumped.

At 11:26 a car went by, headlights smearing across the frame, headed toward the point. Des slowed it down. Dark, small, sort of boxy. The kind of car half the town drives. No plate we could read. It was gone in two seconds.

"Write that down," Des said.

I was already writing it down.

11:30. 11:34. The road doing absolutely nothing, over and over, the most boring footage in the world.

Right up until it wasn't.

Des's hand came off the mouse.

"There," he said, and his voice had gone very quiet. "11:38. Somebody's walking."

A girl came in from the right edge of the frame, on foot, on the shoulder of the road. Head down. Hands in her pockets. A dark jacket.

Then, at 11:39, from the left side of the frame, out of the dark past where the light gave up, somebody else.

I leaned in so close I could smell the dust on the monitor.


# Chapter Six

The girl on the shoulder of the road was Camille.

I knew it before the camera gave us her face, which it never really did. I knew it from the walk. Camille walked like she was late for something even when she wasn't, head down, shoulders forward. I'd sat behind that walk in the hallway between chemistry and English for two years.

She was wearing the jacket. Even in black and white, even grainy, I could see the pale wear across the shoulders.

She was wearing both shoes. White sneakers, bright against the wet road, catching what was left of the store's light.

The other figure came out of the dark on the left side of the frame. Hood up. Hands jammed in the front pocket of a sweatshirt. Walking toward her, toward the light.

Camille stopped. She turned to face whoever it was.

They stood there on the shoulder of the road, maybe three feet apart. Not touching. Camille's arms were crossed. The other person's head was down, then up, then down again. At one point Camille pointed back the way she'd come, toward town. A sharp little stab of her arm.

The timestamp said 11:40.

It lasted about forty seconds. Then Camille shook her head, turned, and walked off the left edge of the frame toward the point. Alone.

The hooded figure stood there a few seconds longer, watching her go. Then it turned and walked back the way it came, out of the light, into the dark.

That was it. That was the whole thing.

"Run it back," I said.

* * *

Des ran it back.

We watched it six more times, slower each time. On the third pass, Des pointed at the far left corner of the screen. Just past where the light gave out, there was a smear of red. Two dots, low to the ground.

"Brake lights," he said. "Somebody's parked on the shoulder."

He scrubbed backward. At 11:35, a car passed going toward the point. Bigger than the first one. A pickup, maybe, or an SUV. It slowed down at the edge of the frame and stopped, and its brake lights stayed on, a dull red glow just barely in the shot.

At 11:43, after the hooded figure walked back into the dark, the brake lights went off. Then a pair of headlights swung around in a U-turn and the car went back past the camera toward town.

"So whoever that was got a ride," Des said. "And their ride waited."

"Which means the ride saw everything."

"Which means there are at least two people who know who that is." He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "Face is nothing, by the way. Hood, angle, the light's doing us zero favors. I could run it through an enhancer but it's going to give us a blob with confidence."

"I don't need the face," I said.

"What do you mean you don't—"

"Watch the walk."

He watched the walk.

It was small. Easy to miss if you weren't looking for something wrong. When the figure turned and walked away, there was a hitch in it. A drag on the right side. The right leg didn't bend quite all the way at the knee, so it swung a little outward on every step, stiff, like the knee had something holding it straight.

Like a brace.

Not dramatic. Not a limp you'd notice at the grocery store. But a limp you'd absolutely notice if you'd spent the last month watching somebody hobble through the halls of Hollow Bay High, complaining to anybody who would stand still about how a torn ligament had cost him the last three games of his senior season.

"That's Theo," I said.

Des didn't argue. He went quiet in the specific way that means he's already three steps past the sentence I just said, filing it, getting the receipts ready.

"Okay," he said finally. "But Theo was at Colby's Monday night. That whole Monday Night Football thing. There's photos of it everywhere. I've literally seen his face in the background of somebody's story."

"When?"

"I don't know when exactly. That's the point. They're just there."

"Then we find out when."

* * *

Delgado knocked on the door frame. "Eleven minutes," he said. "Truck's early."

"Can you save it?" I said. "Not just this part. The whole night. Before it gets erased tomorrow."

He frowned. "Save it how?"

Des was already holding up a USB drive he'd pulled from somewhere in his bag. Des always has a USB drive somewhere in his bag. He says it's like carrying an umbrella. "It'll take like two minutes. Export the whole night, Monday six PM to Tuesday eight AM. And you keep the original too, Mr. Delgado. Like, put it somewhere and don't touch it. In case somebody official asks for it later."

"I thought somebody official said they didn't want it."

"In case they change their mind," I said.

Delgado looked at me for a while. Then at the screen, where the hooded figure was paused mid-step, one leg stiff and swinging.

"That's the Malhotra girl," he said. "Isn't it."

I didn't say anything.

He sighed through his nose, a long one. "She used to buy those sour gummy worms. Every day after practice. Paid in quarters." He nodded at Des. "Do your two minutes."

It took Des four. Delgado copied the file a second time onto his own little drive and put it in the drawer of his desk under a stack of invoices and said he'd leave it there till the end of time, or until somebody with a badge asked him nicely, whichever came first.

We thanked him. I promised this was the last favor I'd ever ask him for. All three of us knew that wasn't true.

* * *

By seven o'clock Sunday night, we were back in my garage with the whiteboard turned around and Des's laptop on the workbench, and we had eleven photos from Colby Reed's house on Monday night.

They hadn't been hard to find. That was the first weird thing.

Colby runs a server for his friends. Everybody knows about it. It's mostly seniors and a few juniors, invite-only, a place to dump party pictures and clips nobody wants on their real accounts. Des and I aren't in it. We've never been in it. But on Tuesday afternoon, a few hours after everyone found out about Camille, people started screenshotting things from it and posting them in the bigger servers. Then people started reposting the screenshots. By Wednesday they were everywhere, with captions like *leave theo alone he was literally at colby's* and *he's going through enough.*

"So we're working from screenshots of screenshots," Des said. "Which is great. I love that for us."

He pulled them up one by one and laid them out on the screen like cards.

Colby's basement. A TV the size of a garage door with a football game on it. An air hockey table. A lot of guys from the soccer team, and some girls, and Kenzie Ochoa in the background of two of them making a peace sign. And Theo, in six of the eleven. Knee brace on over his jeans. Red cup. Grinning in some. Not grinning in others.

The original post, the one everybody had screenshotted, was from Colby. Tuesday, 3:12 PM.

[[LOG]]

colbyreed: for everyone in the comments acting weird. 11:15 theo getting cooked at air hockey

Two of the photos matched those captions. Theo bent over the air hockey table, grinning. Theo sitting on the arm of a couch, cup raised, mouth open like he was yelling at the TV.

In a third photo, from later, you could actually see a phone screen in someone's hand in the foreground. Des zoomed in until it was all squares, then backed up until it was readable. 12:40.

"11:15," Des said quietly. "11:52. 12:40."

"That's a problem."

"That's not a problem. That's an alibi." Des sat back. "If those times are real, he's in Colby's basement the whole time we need him to be on the side of the road."

"The camera has somebody with his exact limp at 11:40."

"Which means either the camera's wrong—"

"The camera isn't wrong."

"—or the party's wrong. And I don't know how a party is wrong, Wren. There were like thirty people there. Thirty people don't fake an alibi together. That's not a thing teenagers are organized enough to do. We can't even organize a group project."

He had a point. I hated that he had a point. It meant I had a boy with exactly the right injury standing in exactly the wrong place at exactly the right time, on camera. And I had a story that thirty people had backed up without anybody seeming to coordinate a single lie.

* * *

I went inside to do the thing I do when I'm stuck, which is walk around the kitchen touching things that don't need touching. The salt shaker. The magnet from the dentist shaped like a smiling tooth. The clock that's four minutes fast.

My dad was at the table doing bills. He has a system with a calculator and a yellow legal pad that he refuses to replace with an app. He watched me straighten a dish towel that was already straight.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

It was the exact tone Des uses. Either I have one very specific stuck-face, or the two people who know me best just happen to speak the same language about it.

"I'm fine."

"You said that on Tuesday too." He set his pen down. "Des been here all weekend for any particular reason?"

"He likes our cheese."

"Wren."

"It's a project." That wasn't a lie. It was a project. "For school. Sort of."

He looked at me for a long time. Long enough that I thought he was going to ask the actual question, the one sitting between us on the table next to the legal pad. I could almost hear him building up to it.

Then he picked his pen back up.

"Don't stay up too late," he said. "Either of you."

That's how it works in my house. He doesn't ask the question, I don't give the answer, and we both get to pretend there wasn't one. I used to think that meant he didn't want to know. Lately I've started to think he wants to know so badly he can't risk hearing it.

I went back to the garage feeling worse than when I'd left it.

* * *

"What if it's not him," Des said when I came back in. He had his head down on the workbench next to the laptop. "What if the limp's a coincidence. What if we're seeing what we want to see because Theo's the easy answer. The boyfriend's always the easy answer."

"Or what if the alibi's the part that's fake, and we just don't know how yet."

"That's a very convenient thing for you to believe, considering it means you get to keep suspecting him."

He wasn't wrong about that either. Everybody around me kept being annoyingly right about my blind spots, and I kept walking straight into them anyway.

"Fine," I said. "We don't decide tonight. We sit with both things. He's got the leg. He's also in photos he couldn't have faked by himself."

"That's not an answer."

"It's not supposed to be an answer yet."

We crossed one thing off, at least. Sasha Kowalski, the relay girl who'd lost the anchor spot to Camille, had been livestreaming herself playing a horror game from eleven to almost one in the morning on Monday. Des found the stream archive in about four minutes. Two hours of Sasha screaming at zombies in her bedroom with a timestamp running in the corner. Whatever Sasha's problem with Camille was, she hadn't been on Hollow Point.

Des drew a line through her name on the whiteboard. It felt like the first solid thing we'd done all day.

"One down," he said.

"Thirty to go."

He groaned into the workbench. "I hate this. I can feel that something's wrong, and I hate that I can feel it. This is how people end up on the news, Wren. Two dumb teenagers who found a clue and didn't tell an adult."

"We told Delgado."

"Delgado doesn't count. Delgado thinks there's a Bigfoot living behind the dumpster."

"There might be."

He lifted his head just far enough to give me a look that said he did not find that funny. The corner of his mouth had already given him away.

* * *

Des went home at ten. He took the USB drive with him, because his laptop has a better video player than mine, and because he said he wanted to go through the whole night properly, every minute, not just the part we already knew about.

"It's fourteen hours of footage, Des."

"At eight times speed it's less than two."

"You have school tomorrow."

"So do you. Look at us, both making terrible choices." He pointed at me from the garage door. "Go to sleep. Brakes. I'm being the brakes right now. This is what brakes look like."

"You're literally about to stay up all night."

"Brakes are for you," he said. "Not me." Then he did the lift, the lean, the clunk, and the shove, and he was gone.

I didn't go to sleep. I lay in bed with the light off and the case log open, rereading what I'd written.

# Chapter Seven

By Monday morning I'd slept about three hours, and I'd watched the 4:58 AM car eleven times on my phone.

It doesn't look like much. That's the worst part. Headlights in the fog, coming from the direction of town. One bright, one dim, like a face with one eye half closed. Then the shape of a small dark car going past the pumps toward the point. Then nothing for twenty-five minutes. Then the same headlights coming back the other way, going a little faster.

Somebody went out to Hollow Point in the dark, the morning after a girl went off it, and came home before anybody knew.

I kept trying to think of a normal reason. A fisherman. Somebody's early shift at the lighthouse, except nobody works at the lighthouse, it's been automated since the nineties. A teenager sneaking home from somewhere. A person who couldn't sleep and liked to drive.

Every normal reason had the same problem. Whoever it was had already been out there once, at 11:26 the night before, and left at 12:16. That's not somebody who likes to drive. That's somebody who forgot something.

I thought about the shoe. The left one sitting upright outside the tape. The right one gone.

I was still thinking about it when I got to my locker and found Priya Malhotra waiting for me.

* * *

She was standing with her back against the locker next to mine, arms crossed. Ponytail back. Not a single strand out of place. She had the same expression she'd had on the cliff on Wednesday, telling me her cousin wasn't going to be my little project.

My first thought was not hope. My first thought was: great, round two.

"I'm not here to fight," she said, before I could open my mouth.

"Okay."

"I mean it. I had a whole weekend to decide whether I still hate you for Wednesday. And I do, a little. But that's not why I'm here."

I opened my locker slower than I needed to, mostly to give myself a second. "Then why are you here?"

She looked up and down the hallway before she answered. That told me more than anything she'd said. Priya doesn't check who's listening. Priya is the one everyone else checks for, because if something matters she'll have it posted on the Beacon by lunch. Her checking meant this wasn't going on the Beacon. Not yet.

"I've been working on a piece," she said. "About Camille. Not how she died. Who she was. Before everybody turned her into a candle on a cliff." Her jaw tightened. "I wanted one honest thing about her out there that isn't a slideshow with a sad song."

"That's a good idea."

"I didn't ask what you thought." But it came out with no heat. More like a reflex. "I went to the library Friday, after the counselors cleared out. To ask Ms. Feiler for the old relay team photo from freshman year. The good one, where Camille's actually laughing, instead of her school picture where she looks like a hostage."

"Okay."

"And Ms. Feiler said something. Kind of offhand, while she was looking for it." Priya's voice stayed level, but I watched her fingers tighten on the strap of her bag. "She said Camille had been in the archive room a few weeks ago. By herself. Reading old newspapers."

I waited.

"From six years ago," Priya said. "The week your sister went missing."

There are sentences that change the shape of a day. That one changed the shape of about four days at once, all of them still on their way.

"Say that again," I said. It's a stupid thing to say when you heard somebody perfectly the first time. My mouth needed something to do while the rest of me caught up.

"Camille. My cousin Camille, who I would bet actual money never once said your sister's name to me in my entire life. She went into the archive and read about Rowan Ashby." Priya's voice cracked, just a little, on Rowan's name. It was the first uneven thing she'd let me see. "Ms. Feiler said she didn't think anything of it at the time. Kids pull weird stuff for projects. But Camille didn't take notes. She just sat there for like forty minutes, reading, and then she left, and she didn't say why."

"When? What week?"

"She didn't remember exactly. End of September. She said she'd have to look at the sign-in sheet." Priya frowned at me. "Why does the week matter?"

It mattered because it would tell me how long Camille had been carrying whatever she'd found before she walked up the road on Monday night in my sister's jacket.

I didn't say that. Not yet. There's a version of me that would have, the version that talks first and regrets it during fourth period. But something in Priya's face stopped me. She looked like someone holding out a piece of broken glass, not sure yet whether it was going to cut her.

"I don't know yet," I said. That was mostly true. "But I don't think it's nothing."

"I didn't think it was nothing either. That's the problem." She shifted her bag on her shoulder. "I spent all weekend trying on boring explanations. A history project. A true crime thing, people do that, they read about old local cases because it's creepy. I've tried like six boring explanations, and none of them fit."

"So you came to me."

"I came to you because apparently you're the only person in this town who has spent six years becoming an expert on one specific missing girl." She said it flat, no apology. I respected that more than I would have if she'd tried to soften it. "And I needed to know if this actually means something, or if I'm about to hand my family's private business to the person with the worst reputation in school for turning other people's tragedies into projects. So. Does it mean something?"

I thought about lying to her. Not a big lie. Something small to buy time, so I could work it alone the way I do everything. But she'd walked over here first. She'd handed me the piece of glass first. There's a kind of person who does that, and a kind who doesn't, and I wanted, for once, to be the kind who didn't waste it.

"Camille was wearing a jacket when she died," I said. "Navy. Kind of old. Plaid lining."

Priya's eyes narrowed. "I know what she was wearing. I've seen her in it a hundred times. She wore it all summer."

"Do you know where she got it?"

"I don't know. Thrifted it? She thrifted everything. Or somebody gave it to her." Priya shook her head. "Camille had a lot of stuff people gave her. People just gave Camille things. Why?"

"Because my sister was wearing the exact same jacket the night she disappeared."

Priya didn't flinch. That surprised me. She just went very still, the way you go still when you've stepped on a stair that isn't there.

"That's a big thing to say to someone in a hallway before first period," she said.

"You asked."

"I did." She breathed out through her nose, long and slow, like she was letting go of an argument she'd been having with herself since Friday. "Okay. Okay. Here's where I am. I still think you'd chase this even if it were a stray dog and not my cousin. I haven't forgotten that. But I can't sit on the one piece of this that doesn't add up just because I don't like who's good at adding things up."

She pulled a folded index card out of her back pocket. The kind she uses for interview questions. The edges were already soft from being carried around all weekend.

"Ms. Feiler's exact words," she said. "As close as I could get them. I wrote them down in the parking lot. In case it matters later. In case somebody asks how we knew."

I took it. "We?"

"Don't make it weird." But it didn't sound like a no. It sounded like two people agreeing to stop making each other's lives worse. "I'm not doing this so we can be friends, Wren. I'm doing this because whatever my cousin was looking for in that archive, she didn't get to finish looking. And I'd like to know what it was before somebody buries it with her."

"I want the same thing."

"I know. That's the only reason I'm standing here." She hesitated. Then, quieter, like it cost her something: "My mom asked me last night why I was even talking to you. Half my family was at my aunt's house, doing funeral stuff, there are like four hundred casseroles in that kitchen. And I said—" She stopped.

"You said what?"

"I said Wren Ashby thinks Camille's jacket means something. Because somebody at the vigil told me you'd been asking Gary Ostrander about it, and the shoe, on Tuesday, before the van even left." She gave me a look. "Were you?"

I had been. Briefly. Gary didn't remember anything about the jacket except that it was "dark." I'd asked him three different ways.

"And it came out wrong," Priya went on. "It came out like I was mad about it. And my aunt kind of looked up, and then everybody looked up, and then my mom started a whole thing about how that Ashby girl needs to leave grieving families alone." She shrugged, jerky. "So. Everybody in that kitchen knows you're asking questions about Camille's jacket. I'm sorry. That's not great for whatever this is."

"Who was there?"

"Everybody. My mom. My aunt. My other aunties. Neighbors. Church people. Half the women in Quiet Hollow bring a dish and stay three hours." She looked at the clock over the trophy case. "Don't make me regret this, Wren."

She walked off toward homeroom before I could answer. That was probably smart. I didn't have a good answer ready.

Nobody in the archive moved.

"I don't know." It was the truest thing I'd said all day. "If it is, somebody had it for six years. Somebody kept it. And then somebody gave it to Camille."

"And Camille figured that out."

"Camille figured out *something*. Three weeks before she died." I pulled my knees up. "And then she went up to a lighthouse she was scared of, in the middle of the night, to meet somebody, and she turned her location off so nobody would see where she went."

Priya was quiet for a long time.

"She asked me in September if I remembered Rowan Ashby," she said. "It was so random. We were in my car. She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. "I should have asked her. I should have asked her why."

"You couldn't have known."

"That's not the point." She dropped her hands. Her eyes were dry, and furious. "She was carrying this by herself. For three weeks. She didn't tell me. She didn't tell anybody. Why wouldn't she tell anybody?"

I didn't have an answer. I could only think of one reason you'd keep something like that to yourself, and it was that you were afraid of the person it led back to.

Or you were afraid nobody would believe you about them.

The bell rang for fifth period. Neither of us got up.

# Chapter Eight

I asked Theo Marsh about the jacket on Tuesday, between second and third period. I'd like to say it was a strategic decision. It wasn't. He was at his locker, and I was walking past it, and the question was out of my mouth before the part of my brain that makes plans could weigh in.

"Hey," I said. "Theo."

He looked up. It took him a second to place me, which is fair. We've had maybe two conversations in our lives, both about a group project.

Theo's did neither.

The color went out of it. All of it, all at once, like somebody had pulled a plug. His hand stopped moving inside the locker. For one long second, he just looked at me.

Then he looked down at his phone. Not like he'd gotten a text. Like he was checking whether it was still there.

"Why are you asking me that?" he said.

"Because I'm asking."

"It's just a jacket." His voice had gone strange and flat. "She just had it. She had it all summer. I don't know. Ask somebody else."

Half the school watched it live in the cafeteria, crowded around phones propped against napkin dispensers. People cluster around bad news the way they cluster around a bonfire. Close enough to feel it. Not so close it burns.

Sheriff Lockhart has a face made for local news. I don't mean that as a compliment. I mean he has the kind of face that reads as trustworthy on a small screen at six o'clock. Square jaw. Easy voice. Silver at the temples in a way that looks deliberate. He's been sheriff since before I was born, and he has the practiced stillness of a man who's stood in front of a camera enough times to forget it's there.

My dad got absorbed into a cluster of mill guys by the window. Nora was running the kitchen. She had an apron on over her black dress and was moving between the stove and the counter with trays, telling the church ladies where things went, refilling the coffee urn, steering people toward the food. She waved at me once over somebody's head and mouthed *you look tired, honey.*

I waved back. Of course she was running the kitchen. That's what Nora does. She shows up and she runs the kitchen.

Priya found me by the corkboard after about an hour. She didn't say anything about Friday. She just stood next to me and looked at the ribbons.

"She was so fast," she said. "It made me so mad. I'm the one who actually trained."

"I'm sorry about yesterday," I said.

"I know you are." She didn't look at me. "I'm still mad."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair. I don't need you to tell me what's fair." But she didn't walk away.

After a while she said, "Come here," and led me down the hall, past the bathroom, to the front entryway where everybody's shoes were piled up on the mat. The Malhotras are a shoes-off house. There was a whole mountain of them. Funeral flats and dress shoes and sneakers.

Off to the side, on a little rack against the wall, there were three pairs on their own. Sneakers. A pair of running flats. Fuzzy slippers shaped like frogs.

Every single one of them had the heel crushed flat. Walked down into the sole, folded under, like somebody had stepped on the back of them every day for years.

"Those are hers," Priya said. "My aunt won't let anyone move them." She crouched and touched the frog slippers. "Camille never untied her shoes. Ever. It drove her mom insane. She'd tie them once, when they were new, and then she'd just step into them and kick them off. She ruined every pair she ever owned. Her mom bought her those expensive white ones in August and said if you crush the heels on these I swear to God."

I looked at the shoes for a long time. The left shoe in the grass, upright. Laces still tied, like somebody had just stepped out of it.

That's exactly what had happened. Camille had stepped out of it. The way she'd stepped out of every shoe she ever owned.

Something had happened on that grass at the top of the stairs. Something that made her foot come out of her shoe and keep going without it.

"Wren," Priya said. "You're doing the face."

"Everybody keeps telling me that."

"Because you keep doing it." She stood up and brushed off her knees. "What is it?"

"I'll tell you," I said. "I promise. I just need to think about it for one more day."

She looked at me for a long moment. I could see her deciding whether to push.

"One day," she said.

* * *

We left around two. My dad was quiet in the truck. Funerals do that to him. I watched the town go by out the window. The harbor, the bait shop, the Fuel Stop, gray and wet in the afternoon.

I kept thinking about the shoes. I barely noticed anything else about the rest of the afternoon. People came and went from that house the whole time we were there. Somebody's uncle left to get more folding chairs from the church. Mr. Okafor from next door went home to let his dog out. Nora took her keys off the hook by the back door, said something to one of the church ladies about ice, and came back in later with a bag on each hip and her cheeks pink from the cold. Colby Reed's mom showed up for ten minutes and left. Nobody keeps track of who comes and goes at a funeral. That's sort of the point of them. Everybody's there, and nobody's watching.

We pulled into our driveway at 2:31.

"Huh," my dad said.

The garage door was up. Not all the way. About two inches. Just enough that there was a dark line of gap along the bottom, between the door and the concrete.

"Did you leave that open?" he said.

"No. You pulled it down this morning. I watched you."

"I thought I did." He frowned at it. "Thing's got a mind of its own." He got out of the truck, walked over, and pushed it down with one hand until it settled against the concrete. It made its long squeal.

He didn't think anything of it. Why would he? Our garage door has been half broken my entire life.

I followed him into the house. I went upstairs to change out of the funeral clothes. I opened my closet door to hang up my dress.

And I looked up at the top shelf, the way I always do, without deciding to.

The shoebox was facing the wrong way.

* * *

It wasn't gone. That took me a second to understand, standing there with the dress still on its hanger in my hand. Nothing was knocked over. Nothing was on the floor. The yearbooks were still in front of it, still in a stack.

It was just turned. The water stain was facing out.

I've been specific about that shelf for six years. The lid seam goes to the wall. The stain goes to the wall. Every single time. I'd put it back that exact way last Saturday afternoon, sitting on my floor with Rowan's poster in my lap. I'd made sure.

Now the stain was the first thing I saw.

I climbed onto my desk chair and took it down. I sat on the floor and opened it with hands that weren't quite steady.

Everything was there. The hair tie. The ticket stub. The guitar pick. The printed photos, all of them, including the one from the bonfire. The poster, folded at the bottom.

But it was in the wrong order. The photos had been on top of the poster. Now the poster was folded on top of the photos, the creases not quite lined up with the old ones. The bonfire print, the one of Rowan in the jacket, was face down. I never leave anything of hers face down.

Somebody had been in here.

Somebody had stood on my chair, taken my sister's things down from my closet, gone through them one by one, looked at the photo of Rowan in the jacket, and put it all back almost right. Close enough that on any other week I would never have noticed.

This wasn't any other week.

I sat very still on my bedroom floor. The house was quiet. Downstairs I could hear my dad running water in the kitchen sink. Rain was starting again, ticking on the window.

And under the smell of my own room, faint, almost not there, there was something else. Something that didn't belong. Clean. Soft. Like somebody else's laundry.

I breathed in, trying to place it, and it was already gone. Maybe it had never been there. Maybe it was just the funeral, the lilies and the coffee and a hundred people's coats, still in my hair.

* * *

There was no broken window. I checked. I checked every window in the house while my dad was in the shower, and every one was latched. The front door had been locked. The back door had been locked.

The door from the garage into the kitchen doesn't lock. It's never locked. It doesn't need to. The garage door is the lock. If you don't know the trick, you can't get it open, and if you yank on it you just make it squeal loud enough to wake the whole block.

But the garage door had been up two inches when we got home.

I stood in the garage and looked at it and made myself think it through.

You lift it about a foot. You lean on the left side until it clunks. You shove. Then you're in, and you close it behind you, and it sticks the way it always sticks, and maybe it doesn't come all the way down if you're in a hurry and don't give it that last push.

Who knew the trick? Me. My dad. Des. Rowan, once.

Des had been standing next to me at the reception for three hours.

Anybody else could have watched Des do it. It's not a secret. Des does it in broad daylight about three times a week. Anybody who'd driven past our house could have seen him.

And whoever had done it had known something else. They'd known that my dad and I would both be gone for hours on a Saturday. They'd known exactly where we'd be. The same place half the town would be.

Everybody who knew that Camille's funeral was Saturday morning knew our house would be empty.

Theo Marsh had walked out of the funeral before the eulogy was even over.

* * *

I called Reyes. Not a text. Some things need a voice.

She picked up on the third ring. "This is a specific number to be calling on a Saturday."

"Somebody was in my house."

Her voice changed completely. Everything wary went out of it and something fast and clipped came in. "Is anybody hurt. Are you safe right now. Where's your dad."

"I'm fine. He's fine. He's in the shower. He doesn't know." I told her. The garage door. The shoebox. The wrong order, the photo face down, nothing taken. "Nothing's broken. There's no sign anybody broke in. That's the part that's freaking me out. Whoever it was didn't need to break anything."

"While you were at the funeral."

"Yes."

She was quiet for a second. I could hear her moving. A door. Keys.

"I'm coming over," she said. "Not as a detective. I can't officially do anything with *a shoebox was facing the wrong way*. You understand that, right? There's no crime here I can write up that Boone won't throw in a drawer. But I want to see it myself. I want to see your locks. And I don't want you to touch anything else in that closet until I do."

"Okay."

"And Wren." Her voice dropped. "Whatever this is, it isn't nothing anymore. A text is one thing. Somebody in your house is a different thing. I need you to actually hear that. Not agree with me to get off the phone."

"I hear it."

"Good. Because your dad needs to hear it too."

I didn't answer.

"Wren."

"I know."

"Before I get there," she said. "You tell him before I get there. I'm not walking into that man's house and telling him somebody's been in his daughter's room, when he doesn't even know his daughter's been looking. I'm not doing that to him. You are."

She hung up without saying goodbye. I was starting to understand that was just how she ended calls.

I sat on my bed with the phone in my lap. Downstairs, the shower turned off. The pipes knocked in the walls the way they always do. My dad's footsteps went across the bathroom floor, slow and heavy, a man tired from a funeral.

For six years, he'd been asking me to stop.

I'd never told him I hadn't.

I stood up and went downstairs.


# Chapter Ten

My dad was in the kitchen in his undershirt and his funeral pants, filling the kettle. His hair was still wet. He looked older than he had that morning. Funerals do that to everyone, I think, but they do it to him more.

"You want tea?" he said, without turning around.

"Dad. I need to tell you something."

He heard it in my voice. I watched him hear it. He turned off the faucet and set the kettle down on the counter, very carefully, like it might go off.

"Okay," he said.

I'd been trying to figure out how to start for the whole length of the stairs. In the end I just started at the beginning, because there isn't any other place to start a thing like that.

I told him about the jacket on the cliff stairs. The lining. The smell. I told him about Rowan's last post and the poster in my shoebox and the line that said *last seen wearing*. I told him about the Fuel Stop camera and the 4:58 car and Camille in the archive asking if they ever found the jacket. I told him about the text on Thursday night. And then I told him about the garage door, and the shoebox, and the photo of Rowan lying face down.

He didn't say anything the whole time. He stood at the counter with his hand flat on it, the way he does, like he was checking whether it was still solid.

When I got to the shoebox, he took his hand off the counter.

"Somebody was in this house," he said.

"Yes."

"Today. While we were at that church."

"Yes."

"In your room."

"Yes."

He walked out of the kitchen. I heard him go up the stairs, fast, two at a time, which I haven't heard him do in years. I heard my closet door. Then nothing for a long time.

When I went up, he was standing in the middle of my room looking at the top shelf of my closet. He didn't touch it. He just looked.

"Reyes is on her way," I said quietly.

"The detective."

"She said I had to tell you before she got here."

He turned around and looked at me. And I'd been braced for a lot of things. Yelling. The jaw thing. The closed laptop. The *I asked you to let this go*.

What I hadn't been braced for was that he'd look scared.

My dad doesn't look scared. My dad looked tired for six years, and sad, and careful, and sometimes angry in the quiet way that's worse than loud. But he never looked scared. Not in front of me. I think he'd decided, when it happened, that one of us had to not look scared, and he'd picked himself.

He looked scared now.

"How long," he said.

"Since the morning she died."

"Two weeks." He sat down on the edge of my bed like his legs had made the decision without him. "Two weeks you've been doing this. And I've been sitting at that table every night."

"I know."

"You looked me in the eye and said you weren't looking for anything."

"I know."

"Wren." He put his face in his hands. When he spoke again, it came out from behind them, muffled. "I can't. I told you I can't do it twice. I meant it. I wasn't being dramatic."

"I'm not Rowan."

He took his hands away from his face and looked at me with red eyes. "You think I don't know that? You think I don't know the difference between my daughters?" His voice cracked straight down the middle. "She went out the door one night and she didn't come back, and I didn't stop her. I didn't even ask where she was going. I was so tired from the double shifts, I was asleep on the couch, and she said *bye Dad* and I said *be careful* without even opening my eyes. That's the last thing. *Be careful.* With my eyes shut."

I'd never heard that before. Not once in six years. I stood in the doorway of my room and didn't move.

"And now somebody's been in your room," he said. "Somebody went through her things, in your room, and I was at a funeral holding a paper plate."

"Dad."

"I'm not mad at you." He laughed, and it wasn't a laugh. "I'm so mad at you. I'm not mad at you. I don't know what I am."

I sat down next to him on the bed. Not touching. Just next to him. The way Des sits next to me.

After a while, he said, "That jacket."

"What about it?"

"She came home in it on the Fourth of July. That summer. It was too warm for it, and she had it on anyway, over a tank top, like a cape. I asked where she got it and she said somebody gave it to her at the bonfire. Said she'd been freezing and somebody gave it to her." He was looking at the floor. "I didn't ask who. I was glad somebody'd been kind to her. I didn't ask a lot of things that summer."

"Somebody gave it to her," I said. "At the Fourth of July bonfire."

"That's what she said." He looked at me. "Why? Does that matter?"

"I don't know yet," I said.

The doorbell rang.

* * *

Reyes was on the porch in her gray rain jacket with her hands in her pockets. She looked at my dad, and my dad looked at her, and something passed between them that I didn't have the words for. Two adults sizing each other up over a kid.

"Mr. Ashby," she said. "I'm sorry to meet you like this."

"You're the one who's been talking to my daughter."

"I'm the one who's been telling your daughter to go home," Reyes said. "She doesn't listen very well."

Something in my dad's face moved, almost. "No," he said. "She doesn't."

He let her in.

She looked at my closet for six minutes. She took photos of the shelf and the shoebox with her own phone, not a department camera. She asked me to show her exactly how I'd found it, and exactly how I'd left it the Saturday before, and she wrote it down in a small notebook. She checked the latch on my bedroom window. She checked the front door, the back door, the windows on the first floor.

Then she went into the garage and stood in front of the door and looked at it for a while.

"Show me," she said.

I showed her. Lift a foot. Lean left. Clunk. Shove.

She tried it. It took her three tries. On the third, it went up with its long complaining squeal and the gray afternoon light came pouring in under it.

"Anybody who's seen it done once could do it," she said. She let it fall shut and turned to my dad. "Who else has watched somebody open this?"

My dad rubbed his jaw. "Des. His mom, probably. Half the neighborhood. The mailman. That door's been broken since—" He stopped. "Since the girls were small."

"Your older daughter knew it."

"Rowan knew it. Everybody who used to come and go knew it."

Reyes wrote that down too. Then she looked at the door that goes from the garage into the kitchen, the one with no lock, and then at my dad.

"I'd put a deadbolt on that tonight," she said.

"I'll go to Kessler's."

"Good." She put the notebook in her pocket. "Mr. Ashby, I'm going to be honest with you, because I think you'd rather have that. I can't open a case on this. Nothing was taken. Nothing was damaged. There's no sign of forced entry. I write this up, it goes in a drawer, and the man who owns the drawer has already told me the thing your daughter's looking into is closed." She paused. "So I'm not writing it up. I'm keeping my own notes. And I'm telling you that I think somebody wanted to know what your daughter has, and came in here to find out."

"And what if they come back?"

"Then you'll have a deadbolt." Reyes looked at me. "And she'll have stopped giving them reasons to."

My dad looked at me too.

They were both looking at me. I've never felt more like a kid in my whole life.

"Who," my dad said. "Who would do this?"

Reyes was quiet for a second. I knew she wasn't going to say Theo's name. She was too careful. She wasn't going to give my dad a name to go drive over to with his funeral tie still in his pocket.

"Somebody who knows this house," she said. "And somebody who knew you'd be gone this afternoon. That's what I've got. That's all I've got."

She left at five. She stopped on the porch, and turned, and said to my dad, low, so I almost didn't hear it:

"She's good at this, you know. That's the problem. If she were bad at it, nobody would care what she has."

My dad didn't answer. He closed the door.

* * *

He went to Kessler's and came back with a deadbolt in a plastic package and spent the whole evening installing it. He swore at the drill. He swore at the strike plate. He measured three times and drilled once and the hole was still a little off. He didn't let me help.

At nine-thirty he was done. He turned the bolt back and forth. It made a heavy, solid sound.

"There," he said.

I was sitting on the garage couch with my knees up. He looked at me across all the junk, the whiteboard turned to face the wall, the workbench, the old bikes.

"I'm not going to tell you to stop," he said. "I've been telling you to stop for six years. I can see where that's gotten me."

"Dad—"

"Let me finish." He put the drill down on the workbench. "I'm going to tell you that if you get a text like that again, I hear about it that night. Not two weeks later. That night. Before the detective. Before Des. Me."

"Okay."

"I don't want okay. I want you to say it like you mean it longer than six seconds."

I looked at him. He'd known. All this time, he'd known about the six seconds.

"I'll tell you," I said. "That night."

He nodded. He picked up the empty deadbolt package and turned it over in his hands. Then he turned to the whiteboard, the one facing the wall, the one he'd bought for fantasy football and used once, and he looked at it for a long time.

He didn't turn it around.

"Goodnight, Wren," he said.

* * *

I told Des everything on Sunday morning, sitting on his back porch with two mugs of the terrible instant coffee his mom keeps for guests who don't know better. The text. The shoebox. Reyes testing the garage door. My dad on the edge of my bed, saying *be careful* with his eyes shut.

Des didn't say much while I talked. From Des, that's its own kind of alarm bell. When I was done, he put his mug down on the railing and looked out at his yard for a while like he was recalculating something.

"Okay," he said. "That settles it."

"Settles what?"

"We need Theo's alibi broken, and we need it broken now. Because whoever's going in your house isn't going to wait for us to be careful."

"I didn't tell you so you'd do something reckless."

"I know you didn't. I'm doing it anyway." There was an edge in his voice I don't hear often. Past his usual anxious-funny. Closer to actually angry. "Somebody went through your dead sister's things while you were at a funeral, Wren. I don't care about being careful anymore. I care about this being over."

I should have talked him out of it. I've thought about that a lot since. The version of that Sunday where I said no, let's slow down, and none of what came next happens.

But I didn't say that. Because some ugly, honest part of me needed him to be angry for me exactly as much as he was. So I let him be.

* * *

Here's what we knew, and what I didn't fully understand yet.

Colby Reed's server had a pinned message at the top of the photos channel. It was a link to a shared album. That's how a lot of friend groups do it. Discord shrinks your photos and strips half the information out, so people just dump the real full-size ones into a shared album and post the link. Everyone in the server can see it. Everyone in the server can add to it.

And a shared album shows you things the screenshots don't. You tap a photo, you hit the little *info* button, and it tells you when the photo was actually taken. Not when it was posted. Not what the caption says. When the camera clicked.

Des wasn't in Colby's server. Neither of us was. We've never run in Theo's circle and we'd never needed to.

But a private server only stays private as long as nobody inside decides to let someone in. And Des spent all of Sunday afternoon working an angle on a sophomore named Eli Brandt. Eli is on JV swim and was at Colby's on Monday night, because his older brother is on the soccer team and dragged him along. Des knew him a little from robotics club freshman year. After two days of polite, mostly harmless pestering, Eli added an account Des had made under a name that wasn't his. No questions asked. The way fifteen-year-olds let people into things all the time without thinking twice about who's asking.

He texted me Tuesday during lunch. Three words.

[[LOG]]
Des: found something. maybe.
[[/LOG]]

"Yes."

"They're in the shared album. The originals. Look."

I stared at the screen.

"Not 11:15," Des said. "12:21. Not 11:52. 12:38." He said it carefully, like he didn't trust the words yet either. "Like an hour off, both of them. Same direction."

"Could the phone's clock be wrong?"

"That's what I thought. That happens. Phones get weird." He scrolled. "But there's like sixty other photos in this album from Monday night, from like fifteen different people's phones. I checked a bunch of them. The times all line up with each other. If Colby's phone was wrong, his would be off from everybody else's. And it's not. His other photos match everybody's." He sat back. "His clock's fine. His captions aren't."

"So the photos are real. He just lied about when he took them."

"He took two photos of Theo after Theo came back. And then on Tuesday afternoon, after everybody found out, he posted them with fake times on them so everybody would screenshot them and pass them around. And they did." Des shook his head slowly. "He didn't have to fake anything. He just had to write a caption. Nobody checks a caption."

"There aren't any photos of Theo from before midnight?"

"There's one at 10:47, him on the couch, and then nothing with him in it until 12:21. That's an hour and a half gap." Des looked at me. "11:40's right in the middle of it."

I wanted to hug him. I didn't, because we don't really do that. Eight years of friendship built on never being sincere about anything for more than four seconds at a time. But I wanted to.

"Screenshot those," I said.

"I did. But screenshots are screenshots. If I'm handing this to Reyes, I want the actual files. Downloaded. The original things, with all the data still in them. Otherwise it's my word and a picture of a screen."

"How long does that take?"

"Not long. I'll do it tonight. It's a lot of photos and I want all of them, not just two, so nobody can say I cherry-picked." He closed the laptop. "Tonight. Then we've got it."

* * *

That's when it went wrong.

I only got the whole story later, in pieces. Des doesn't like telling stories about himself where he doesn't come out looking at least a little competent. He told me this one looking at his own hands the whole time.

Colby runs that server. He keeps half an eye on it. And on Tuesday night, around ten, Colby noticed an account he didn't recognize downloading every single photo from Monday night out of the shared album. One after another, fast, all of them. The kind of pattern that doesn't look like a bored kid scrolling. It looks exactly like what it was.

Colby removed the account from the album before the download finished. Then he looked up who'd added it to the server. Then he got Eli Brandt on a call.

Eli gave up Des's name almost immediately. Des was very clear about this part. He insisted on it. Not because Eli was mean. Because he was fifteen and getting yelled at by a call full of seniors, and he did what you do when you're fifteen and scared, which is hand over whoever got you into it as fast as you can.

They found Des in the student lot after school on Wednesday.

Three of them. Colby and two soccer guys. They didn't touch him. They didn't do anything anyone could have pointed to later and called an incident. They just stood close enough, around his car, and said enough. Des described it afterward as the scariest completely nonviolent conversation of his entire life.

"They knew everything," he told me. We were sitting in his car in my driveway. He hadn't turned the engine off. His voice was flat in a way I didn't like. "They knew I was the account. They knew what I was downloading. Colby said, and I'm quoting, because I've replayed this about forty times, *You should think real hard about whose business you're in. Some of us have older brothers who don't think as carefully as we do.*"

"That's a threat."

"It's a threat shaped like a compliment about my thinking skills, yeah." He tried to smile. It didn't land. From Des, that told me more than anything. "I laughed it off. I said something dumb, I don't even remember what. Then I got in the car and sat in the lot for twenty minutes before I could drive, because my hands wouldn't stop shaking."

He held them up. They were steady now. He looked at them like they belonged to somebody else.

"I've never been scared like that, Wren," he said. "Not this whole time. Not the camera, not the car at five AM. That stuff felt like a puzzle. This felt like a door. Like I'd opened a door and there was a room behind it with people in it."

I didn't know what to say. That happens to me almost as rarely as it happens to him. So for once we just sat there in the car not saying anything, looking out the windshield at my garage door.

"I need to step back," he said finally. "Not forever. I'm not bailing on you. I need you to hear that part specifically, because I know how it's going to sound. But I can't be the guy pulling files off a server with three guys in it who apparently have older brothers. Not right now."

"Okay."

"That's it? Okay?"

"You just told me you were scared in a way you've never been scared before, and you're asking me if it's okay to be careful. I'm not going to make you argue me into that." It cost me something to say it that easily. I think he could tell. But I meant it. "I got you into this. I don't get to be mad that you found the edge."

"I hate that you're being reasonable. It's very unlike you."

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain like four moods, and reasonable isn't usually one of them." But he almost laughed. Actually laughed, a little, the first real one since he'd started talking. Something in my chest let go a notch.

"I'm not gone," he said. "Research. Stuff that doesn't put me in a parking lot with anybody's brother. I'm just not doing *that* again."

"That's more than fair."

He drove home. I stood in the driveway and watched his taillights go, both of them equally bright, and thought about the screenshots on his phone. Two photos, an hour off. The proof that Theo Marsh's alibi was a caption and nothing else.

Screenshots. Not files. Not the real thing. Not something that would survive Reyes raising one eyebrow at it.

And the only people who still had the originals now knew exactly who'd been looking.

There had to be another way into that album.

It was going to have to be mine.

# Chapter Eleven

Kenzie Ochoa lost her retainer again on Halloween.

I know that sounds like the setup to a joke. It's not. It's the most important thing that happened that week, and it happened because Kenzie Ochoa cannot keep a piece of molded plastic in her mouth for more than five consecutive days.

She found me at my locker Friday morning. She was dressed as a strawberry. Most of the junior class had dressed up as something, because the school lets you on Halloween as long as you don't bring anything shaped like a weapon. Kenzie was a strawberry with green felt leaves on her head and little black sequins sewn on for seeds, and her face was very serious.

"Wren," she said. "I'm not asking. I'm begging."

"I don't do retainers anymore."

"Fifteen dollars. Des said fifteen."

"Des is not my agent."

"He kind of is, though." She looked at me with enormous, desperate strawberry eyes. "My mom said if I lose another one, she's making me pay for the replacement out of my own money, and it's three hundred dollars, Wren. Three hundred dollars. I'll be paying for it until I die."

Kenzie was at Colby's on Monday night. She was in two of the photos in the background, making a peace sign. Which meant she was in Colby's server. Which meant she could see the shared album.

"Okay," I said. "Where'd you have it last?"

* * *

It took me forty minutes. It was in the pocket of her driving instructor's car. "Because I think somebody lied about when some of those photos were taken. And I need the real ones. The original files, not screenshots."

"Because he wasn't." She said it simply, like it was obvious. "Theo wasn't there the whole time. He was gone for like forty minutes. Maybe more. Colby said he was throwing up in the side yard. "I didn't think about it until everybody started saying Theo was there all night. And then I thought about it a lot. But I didn't want to be the one to say something, you know? Her eyes went wide. "Like a real one?"

She talked to Theo on Monday.

Voluntarily. That was the word she kept using, carefully, when she called me after. A voluntary conversation, at his house, with his mom present, just a few questions about some photo timestamps.

It's disconnected." She said the next part with real frustration, like the number had personally insulted her. "Whoever had it bought it, used it, and threw it away. She stood up. She stood there for a second like she was deciding whether there was anything else worth saying.

There wasn't, apparently.

She just looked at me. Not angry anymore, exactly. More like something had gone out. Some version of hope she'd been quietly keeping since the archive, that this could turn into something honest between us.

She walked out of the library.

I sat at the leaky table for a long time after she left. The library felt bigger than it had ten minutes earlier, and a lot emptier.

Ms. Feiler came by at some point with her cart of books to reshelve. She didn't say anything. She left a cough drop on the table next to my hand, the honey kind, and kept going.

Here's where I was, if I'm being as honest as Priya had just made me be.

Theo had an alibi that was provably fake, and a lawyer who made him untouchable. The one number that might have told me who Camille went up there to meet had scrubbed itself out of existence on purpose. Des had a real, reasonable fear keeping him away from the one place I needed him. And the person who'd spent three weeks cautiously deciding to trust me had just told me, correctly, that I hadn't deserved it.

I walked home without texting anybody. For me, that's rare enough to notice.

My dad's truck was in the driveway. The photo of Rowan was in its place at the bottom of the stairs, dusted, straight, the way it always is. And for the first time I could remember, I looked at it and didn't feel the regular ache of missing her.

Scared that Priya was right. That under all the good reasons I had for doing this, there was a smaller, uglier one sitting at the bottom. That I'd been using people. Des, Priya, Kenzie, Delgado, Reyes. Using them the way you use a ladder, and then stepping off at the top without looking back down.

There's a person in my life who did that once. Who used up a house and a husband and a four-year-old, and then took the good suitcase and stepped off without looking down.

I've spent twelve years being sure I'm not her.

I opened the case log that night anyway. Not writing it down felt like its own kind of lie, and I'd done enough of those for one week.

# Chapter Twelve

I didn't sleep that night. That wasn't news anymore. By then not sleeping was less an event and more a baseline. But this time it wasn't the case keeping me up.

It was Priya's voice, saying the same handful of sentences on a loop I couldn't turn off.

*A useful cousin. A flashlight to look around your own house.*

Around one in the morning I got up. No plan. Just the specific restlessness that makes you walk instead of lie there. I went down the hall to the bathroom and got a glass of water I didn't want and stood there drinking it in the dark.

On the way back, I stopped outside a door I hadn't opened since August.

Rowan's room.

* * *

My dad keeps it the way it was. Not shrine-exactly. No candles, no photos arranged on the bed. Just untouched, the ordinary way a room stays untouched when nobody can stand to decide what happens to it next.

I go in twice a year. Her birthday in April. And the anniversary in August, if you can call six identical Augusts an anniversary instead of a wound that opens on schedule.

I hadn't planned on going in. I want that clear. I found myself standing there with my hand on the doorknob, and some part of me that had been building toward this ever since the shoebox finally won an argument with the part of me that had spent six years staying out.

I went in.

It smelled like nothing. That's always the strangest part. You'd think a room like that would smell like something, like her, like memory. But six years is a long time, and rooms don't hold onto people the way you want them to. It smelled like dust and carpet and the specific staleness of air that doesn't get let out.

I turned on the lamp on her desk instead of the overhead light. The bulb was the old yellow kind. It made everything look like a photograph of itself.

The bedspread, faded in the stripe where the afternoon sun comes through the blinds. The corkboard over her desk, still pinned with ticket stubs and a fourth-place ribbon from a track meet she complained about for a week. A stack of paperbacks on the windowsill, spines cracked white, which means somebody actually read them. A water glass on the nightstand, empty, with a ring of dust inside it, because even rinsing it out would have been a decision.

I don't know what I was looking for. Maybe nothing. Maybe just proof, one way or the other, that Priya was wrong about me. That I was in here because I missed my sister. Not because I was still working an angle, even at one in the morning, even here.

I sat on the edge of her bed. The mattress gave the same way it used to when I'd climb in with her during thunderstorms, and she'd groan and say *fine, gremlin, but if you kick me you're out*, and then read to me in a terrible fake British accent until I fell asleep. She read me the whole first Harry Potter that way one winter. Every character sounded like the same drunk duke.

I'd forgotten about the accent until I sat on her bed. That's how it works with Rowan. The memories don't come when I go looking. They come when I sit down.

I sat there a long time.

Then I got down on my knees and looked under the bed, because that's where you look. That's where everybody keeps the things they don't want found by accident.

* * *

It was a plain cardboard box, dented on one corner, pushed all the way back against the wall where you'd only find it if you were actually looking and not vacuuming.

I pulled it out slowly with both hands, like it might come apart if I moved too fast. I sat on the floor with my back against her bed and opened it.

It wasn't dramatic. That's the strangest part, looking back. I'd built this moment up in my head for years without admitting I was building anything. And what was actually in the box was just a person's ordinary life.

A retainer case, empty. I almost laughed. A friendship bracelet with half the beads gone. A notebook, mostly blank. A few pages of doodles. One page of song lyrics she'd been trying to write, crossed out twice, the third try just the word *ugh*.

And photos. A whole stack. Not printed ones. Instant ones, the square kind with the white border. Rowan had an instant camera that last summer, a mint green one she'd bought with her own money. She said real photos should exist somewhere besides a phone. She took it everywhere. It's not in this room. It's one of the things that went with her.

I went through them one at a time. Rowan at the beach, squinting. Rowan and a friend I didn't recognize making faces. Rowan at a school dance with her hair done up in a way I'd never seen in any picture that made it onto her account. Like this was a version of her that existed only in the parts of her life she didn't perform for anybody.

She'd written on some of the white borders in Sharpie. *june gloom.* *jess's bday!!* *the gremlin learns to swim.* That one was me, eight years old, in water wings, furious.

Then one that made me stop.

Rowan at night, by a fire. The Fourth of July. I knew it was the Fourth because the sky behind her had a smear of fireworks in it, overexposed, a white streak. She was holding the camera out at arm's length, selfie-style. And she was wearing the jacket.

The navy jacket. Brand new. Stiff, the collar not flipped yet. The canvas so dark it was almost black, before years and salt wore it pale. It was hanging off her shoulders like a cape, too big for her, the sleeves pushed up. She was grinning like she'd been given a present.

On the border, in Sharpie: *hand-me-down day!! 🔥 finally warm.*

Behind her, out of focus, the bonfire beach. Shapes of people. Sparklers. Somebody's arm at the very edge of the frame, mostly cut off, a dark sleeve with the cuff turned back, holding a paper plate.

The first day she wore it. The day somebody gave it to her. My dad had said the Fourth. Here it was.

I looked at it for a long time. Long enough that the lamp started to buzz. Then I set it on the carpet next to me, carefully, face up, and kept going.

* * *

Near the bottom of the stack there was one more.

It was the bonfire again. Not the Fourth. Later in the summer, I thought, because the jacket wasn't new anymore. The collar was flipped up now, the way she always wore it. The canvas had started to soften. It was night, and the firelight turned everything orange and soft and gold.

Rowan was laughing at something, caught mid-motion, head tipped back. One arm was slung around someone standing close beside her.

Except there was no one beside her.

Not anymore.

Where a second person should have been, a face, a shoulder, there was a ragged gap. A piece of the photo physically torn away. Not in one rip. Carefully. Little by little, in small bites along the edge, like somebody had sat with it and taken their time removing exactly one person and nothing else.

Her arm was still there, reaching into the gap. Her hand, resting on a shoulder that wasn't there.

I sat on the floor and looked at the space where a person used to be.

Whoever had done this hadn't wanted to destroy the photo. They'd wanted to keep it. Keep Rowan in it. Keep the fire, the jacket, the laugh. And take out only the one thing they couldn't stand to look at.

That's not what you do to a picture of a stranger. That's what you do to a picture of someone who mattered enough to still hurt.

I didn't know who it was. I still don't, not for certain, not as I'm writing this. Somebody tall, from the angle of her arm. Taller than her. That's all the photo would give me.

And I didn't know who'd torn it. Rowan, in a fight, with somebody she'd been close to that summer? Or somebody else, after. Somebody who'd come into this room when she was already gone and found this and couldn't stand it.

I heard the floor creak in the hall before I heard my dad's voice.

* * *

"Wren?"

I didn't move. I didn't have it in me to hide what I was doing. Not that night. He stood in the doorway in his T-shirt and pajama pants and looked at the room. The lamp on. The box open. Me on the floor of his daughter's room at one-thirty in the morning holding a photo I clearly wasn't supposed to have found.

I braced for it. I'd earned it. Six years of an unspoken rule, broken in one night.

It didn't come.

He came and sat down on the floor next to me instead. Slowly, like his knees didn't love the idea. He looked at the photo in my hands for a long time without reaching for it.

"I forgot that was under there," he said finally, quietly. "I think I put it there myself. I don't remember doing it. That whole first year is mostly gone. I remember pieces. I don't remember the doing of things."

"Dad," I said. "Who's this. Who was standing here."

He looked at the torn edge for a long time. Longer than was comfortable. When he spoke, his voice had gone somewhere I didn't recognize. Somewhere careful. Like a man walking across a frozen lake, testing every step.

"I don't know," he said.

It was the first time in six years I'd heard my father say something that sounded like a lie.

Not because his voice cracked, or his eyes moved, or any of the things people say liars do. It was because I know what the truth sounds like coming out of him. I've been listening to it my whole life. Short. Tired. Plain as a board.

This wasn't that.

I didn't push. I still don't fully know why. Maybe it was one in the morning, and neither of us had the strength for what pushing would cost. Or maybe some part of me was scared of what would happen to both of us if I actually got an answer. So I just sat there with him. The two of us on the floor of a room we'd both spent six years avoiding, looking at a photo of a girl who used to live in this house, and a hole where somebody else used to be.

"I miss her every day," he said after a while. He wasn't looking at me. "I know I don't act like it. I know staying out of this room looks like I've moved on. I haven't, Wren. I haven't moved on from one single day of it. I just don't know how to be in it without drowning. And you were so small when it happened. Somebody in this house had to keep their head above water long enough to make you dinner."

"You don't have to explain that."

"I think I do, actually. I think I've owed you that for six years and never said it." He looked at me. There was something in his face I hadn't seen since before. A door standing open that he usually kept shut on principle. "I'm not ready to talk about who that was in the photo. I need you to hear that as the truth, and not as me hiding something. Even though I know it sounds exactly the same either way."

"Okay," I said.

It wasn't okay. We both knew it wasn't. But it was what I had.

"I love you," he said. "I don't say that enough either. Tonight's making me realize how much I don't say."

"I love you too."

We sat there a while longer. The box open between us. The lamp buzzing. And for the first time since the shoe in the wet grass, I let myself just be sad instead of useful. No theory. No timeline. No next step. Just a girl on the floor of her sister's room with her father next to her. Both of us finally in the same place at the same time, instead of circling it from opposite sides.

* * *

I put the torn photo back in the box before I left. Carefully. The way you'd put back something you weren't sure you were allowed to have found. Some part of me understood, even then, that it wasn't mine to take yet. Whatever it meant, it belonged to a story I hadn't earned the end of.

I put the Fourth of July photo back too, on top of the stack, where I'd find it first next time.

My dad slid the box back under the bed, all the way to the wall, exactly where it had been. He turned off the lamp. We stood in the dark doorway for a second, both of us looking back into the room.

Then he closed the door, and we went to bed, and I lay there with my eyes open until the window started going gray.

The last thing I remember thinking before I finally fell asleep was the white border of an instant photo, and Rowan's handwriting in Sharpie, two exclamation points like she was shouting it.

*hand-me-down day.*


# Chapter Thirteen

I woke up at 6:58 with my face mashed into my pillow and the sentence already in my head, like it had been sitting there all night waiting for me to open my eyes.

*hand-me-down day.*

Rowan's caption on her last post. *In my favorite hand-me-down. Never giving this back.*

The Sharpie on the instant photo. *Hand-me-down day!!*

You don't call something a hand-me-down if you bought it. You don't call it that if a boyfriend gave it to you either. A boyfriend gives you a hoodie, and you call it his hoodie, and you wear it until you break up and then you burn it in a sink. A hand-me-down is something that was somebody else's first. Somebody older. Somebody who'd already had it, already worn it, and passed it down.

Except the jacket in the instant photo wasn't old.

That's what I'd seen last night without seeing it. The canvas was dark, almost black. The collar was stiff, not flipped yet. It hung off her shoulders in big square folds like it had just come out of a bag. That jacket had never been worn by anyone before the Fourth of July.

So it wasn't a hand-me-down in the regular sense. It was a joke. Rowan being Rowan. Somebody older had handed her a brand-new jacket, and she'd called it a hand-me-down, because the person who gave it to her was the kind of person who hands you things. Like an aunt. Like a neighbor. Like a grown-up.

I've tried to explain to Des what it feels like when a case turns over. He says it sounds like a panic attack. It's not, exactly. It's more like when you've been looking at one of those optical illusions, the vase that's also two faces, and you've only ever been able to see the vase. I'd been looking at the vase for three weeks.

Camille had believed that. I had believed that. All of it built on one thing.

That there was only one jacket.

So the jacket on Camille might not be Rowan's at all. It might be the other one. The twin. The one on the arm with the paper plate.

Which meant Rowan's jacket was still missing.

I sat with that for a while, because it hurt in a way I wasn't expecting. Some part of me had been hoping. If somebody had Rowan's jacket, then somebody knew where it had been. Somebody had touched it after. Somebody could be found and asked.

We walked back down the gravel path to the truck, the three of us. Behind us the beam swept out over the water and came back again, marking exactly where the rocks start, the way it always has.

I didn't look back at the railing.

I'd seen it.


# Chapter Eighteen

Reyes came by the next afternoon, the way she'd said she would. She sat at our kitchen table and took my statement, and my dad's, and then she put her pen down and told me what she thought of the previous night.

It took about four minutes. She didn't raise her voice once. It was the worst four minutes of my life, and that includes the morning on the cliff stairs.

When she was done, she picked the pen back up.

"Also," she said, "it was good work."

My dad, at the counter, made a sound that might have been a laugh.

"Both of those things are true," Reyes said. "I don't need you to pick one." She looked at me. "Your friend Des says that a lot, apparently."

"He does," I said.

"He's right."

* * *

The rest came in over the next two weeks, the way the end of a case comes in. Not all at once. Piece by piece, like the tide.

The swab from the railing came back from Portland. The blood was Nora's.

The shoe in the Kessler's bag had Camille's DNA inside it, and rust on the toe that matched the railing, and two of Nora's fingerprints on the heel, right where you'd grab somebody's foot if they were falling.

The carrier records for the number ending in 4417 showed every text between that phone and Theo's, going back eleven months. Reyes didn't tell me what was in them. I didn't ask. The prepaid cards that kept the number running had been bought at Harbor Foods, four of them, with a rewards card registered to Nora Petrakis, because Nora never once in her life passed up the chance to save forty cents on a gallon of gas.

The phone itself was in the console of the green wagon, under a packet of tissues. Dead.

The medical examiner revised her report. She said Camille's injuries were consistent with death within moments of the fall. Reyes told me that one in person, sitting on our porch steps, with her coffee going cold beside her.

"It means she probably couldn't have been saved," Reyes said. "Even if Nora had called the second it happened."

I thought about that. "But she didn't know that."

"No," Reyes said. "She didn't know that. She didn't go down there and find out. She didn't call anybody who could have. She sat in her garage for two hours deciding which version to believe." She turned her mug in her hands. "The ME's report doesn't change what Nora did. It just changes what Camille went through. And I'm glad, for her family, that it was fast. That's all it means."

* * *

Nora Petrakis was formally charged eleven days after the night at the point.

It took that long for the county to sort out everything she'd done, because it turned out to be a lot of things, and none of them was the one thing the town had been whispering about. She hadn't pushed Camille. The evidence agreed with her about that. The shoe in her hand, the angle of the fall, the broken stone where Camille's heel had caught, all of it.

The DA's statement was two pages long. Priya posted the whole thing on the Beacon without changing a word, with a note at the top that said this was the last thing she would ever post about Nora Petrakis.

Sexual abuse of a minor. Multiple counts. That was first, and it was the longest part. Then tampering with physical evidence, for the shoe. Tampering with a witness, for the 5:47 text and everything after, for telling a seventeen-year-old what to say and making him afraid not to say it. Burglary, for the afternoon of the funeral, for walking into our house through a garage door my sister had named after her. Hindering prosecution. Obstruction. And a line at the end that said the DA's office was still reviewing the circumstances of the death itself and that additional charges were possible.

"Is it enough?" I asked Reyes.

She thought about it for a long time. She always does. I like that about her.

"It's a lot," she said. "It's going to be a long time before she's anywhere but a courtroom or a cell." She looked out at our street. "Is it enough? I don't think anything's enough. That's not my department. My department is making sure it's true."

* * *

Sheriff Lockhart gave a second press conference. It was shorter than the first one. Thirty-two seconds. I timed it.

"New evidence came to light," he said, on the courthouse steps, in front of the same cameras. Like evidence has legs and walks into a department on its own, instead of being dragged in by a detective he'd spent a month trying to stop, and a gas station owner, and a hardware store clerk, and a girl dressed as a strawberry, and two juniors and a senior sitting on the floor of a school archive.

He thanked "the hard work of this department." He didn't say Reyes's name. He didn't take any questions.

Reyes stood at the very back of the crowd, in her gray rain jacket, holding a coffee she wasn't drinking. When it was over, she caught me looking at her and shrugged.

Later, on our porch, she told me something I've thought about a lot since.

"He asked me for the whole file," she said. "The day after. Everything. Every note, every photo, every interview." She frowned at her mug. "Which is normal. He's the sheriff. But he asked twice if there was anything in it about your sister. About Rowan. He wanted to know if Nora said anything about that summer."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him it was all in the file." She looked at me sideways. "It is. Mostly."

I didn't ask what *mostly* meant. I thought I knew. Nora on the bench, saying *she asked me if I could keep a secret, if it wasn't hers*. I'd told Reyes that part on the walk back to the truck that night. I'd watched her decide what to do with it.

"Why did he want to know?" I asked.

"I've been here fifteen months," Reyes said. "I've learned that in Quiet Hollow, it's never just one reason." She finished her coffee, cold, in one swallow, and made a face. "Go do your homework, Wren."

* * *

The town did what towns do. It talked about it nonstop for exactly as long as it was new, and then it quietly filed it on the shelf of things you don't bring up at barbecues. The same shelf my sister has been sitting on for six years.

Everybody knew about Theo within two days. Nobody released anything to a bunch of high schoolers. But news moves through the servers faster than any official announcement, and by that Friday half the school knew why a grown woman had been arrested and what she had to do with Theo Marsh.

And they got it wrong. That's the part that made me angriest. Not the gossip. The words.

*Theo's affair.* *Theo was hooking up with some old lady.* *Did you know Theo was sleeping with Camille's babysitter, that's so messed up.* Like it was something he did. Like he'd gone looking for it.

Kenzie Ochoa said *ew, Theo* in the hallway, loud, and I stopped walking.

"He was sixteen," I said.

She blinked at me. "I know, that's why it's so—"

"No. Listen. He was sixteen when it started. She was forty-seven. She was in his house every day taking care of his grandma. That's not him doing something gross, Kenzie. That's something somebody did *to* him."

Kenzie looked at me for a long time. Her face changed.

"Oh," she said quietly. "I didn't think about it like that."

"Nobody does," I said. "That's the problem."

She thought about it. Then she went and said it to the girls she'd been talking to. I heard her, down the hall. *No, actually, think about it.* Kenzie isn't a deep person. But she's a person who notices when a dot isn't moving, and then says so out loud.

It doesn't mean Theo did nothing. He lied. He let Camille's family believe she'd gone up there alone. He helped build an alibi out of captions and kept his mouth shut for three weeks while a whole town called his girlfriend's death an accident. The DA's office decided not to charge him with anything, after his statement, and after what his new lawyer called *the full context of his situation*. But not being charged isn't the same as not having done anything. I think Theo knows that better than anybody. I think he's going to know it for a long time.

He didn't come to school for most of that week. When he came back, he was quieter. People left him alone, in a way that was different from before. Less careful. More like they didn't know what to say, which was honest, at least.

He found me at my locker on the Thursday. He stood there with his hands in his hoodie pocket, knee brace on, looking at the floor.

"Priya said you figured out the time," he said. "The 5:47."

"Yeah."

"I looked at that text every day for three weeks. Every day. And I never—" He shook his head. "I never once thought about what time it was."

"You weren't supposed to," I said. "That's sort of the point. She knew you wouldn't."

He was quiet for a while.

"Do you think Cam hated me?" he said. "At the end?"

I'd known he was going to ask. Priya told me he'd asked her too, and she'd said she didn't know. I'd been thinking about it ever since.

"She texted you not to come," I said. "And when you came anyway, she told you to go back. Over and over. Even though she was furious." I looked at him. "I think she was trying to keep you out of it. I think she was going up there to end it for you."

Theo didn't say anything. His jaw worked. He nodded, once, at the floor.

Then he limped off down the hallway, and I watched him go, the right leg swinging out stiff on every step. I'd watched that walk on a screen eleven times. This was the first time I'd watched it and seen a kid.

* * *

I found Priya in the library on a Tuesday, at the leaky table, laptop open. She was working on something with the specific focus she gets when a piece actually matters.

"What are you writing?"

"The thing I said I wanted to write. Before any of this." She turned the screen so I could see.

*Who Camille Malhotra Actually Was.*

Underneath it was a draft full of small, specific, unglamorous details. The group project fight. Her relay splits. The crushed heels. The poem about the ocean. The way she reread every text twice before she sent it. A thing about ranch dressing that three different people had told Priya about, separately, without being asked. Like it mattered more than anything else they could say.

"I'm not putting any of the other part in," Priya said. "Not Nora. Not Theo. Not the point. She doesn't need to be a true crime footnote in her own memorial. She gets to just be a person who put ranch on everything, including pizza. Which is deranged, for the record. But it was hers."

"That's a good piece."

"I know it's a good piece. I've been working on it for two weeks." She looked up at me. There was something easier in her face than there'd been since the vigil. Not healed. Maybe never healed. But easier. "I'm not saying we're friends now. I don't even know what we are. Given how we met and everything since."

"That's fair."

"I know it's fair." Almost a smile. "But I don't hate you anymore. I want that on the record, since apparently everybody in your life has to say things out loud to you or you assume the worst."

"I do assume the worst."

"You really do. It's kind of your whole thing." She looked back at her screen. "For what it's worth, I don't think you were only ever using Camille to get back to your sister. I think both were true the whole time, like Des said, and I was too angry that week to let them both be true at once. I've had two weeks to sit with it." She paused. "I still think you owe me honesty about which one wins when they don't line up. But that's not today's conversation."

"No," I said. "Not today's."

"And Wren." She didn't look up. "When you start on Rowan. For real. I want in."

I didn't know what to say.

"I'm good at archives," Priya said. "I'm good at records. And you're going to need somebody who doesn't let you keep them on need-to-know." She typed a sentence, deleted it, typed it again. "Also, Camille died thinking something happened to your sister that nobody ever answered. I'd like to answer it. For her."

I sat down across from her. We didn't say much after that. Just two people sharing a leaky table in a library, which we hadn't managed once since this whole thing started. It felt like something small and real getting rebuilt, one ordinary Tuesday at a time.

* * *

Des found me at my locker after. He leaned against the one next to it like he'd been standing there for hours instead of the four seconds it actually took him to get there.

"So," he said. "We solved a mur—"

"Don't."

"—a *case*. We solved a case. I was going to say case."

"You were going to say murder."

"I was going to say murder," he admitted. "And then my brain caught up and realized it wasn't one. Which is somehow worse? I don't know what to call it."

I thought about that. I'd been thinking about it for two weeks.

"It's the wrong kind," I said.

He looked at me. "Of what?"

"Of anything. It's not a murder and it's not an accident. Somebody didn't push her, and somebody also didn't save her. And then lied about it to everybody, including a kid." I closed my locker. "There's not a word for that. It's just the wrong kind."

Des was quiet for a while, walking beside me down the hall. Backpack half zipped. Hair sticking up in the back.

"You okay?" he said. "For real. Not the *fine* you say to everybody including yourself."

I thought about it honestly, which took a second, because I hadn't actually asked myself that in a long time.

"I don't know yet," I said. "I solved it. Start to finish. And it doesn't feel like I thought it would. Camille's still gone. Priya's still sad. Nora's still a person who really did love her, and it didn't stop her from anything." I shoved my hands in my pockets. "Nothing closes as clean as it's supposed to."

"Yeah. I don't think it's supposed to." He bumped my shoulder with his. The closest thing to a hug either of us does on a school day. "That's what actually solving something is like. As opposed to how it looks on TV."

"When did you get wise?"

"Twenty-six minutes, staring at a dot," Des said. "It aged me. I'm basically eighty now."

We walked out into the parking lot. It was cold and bright, the kind of November day where the sky is so clean it looks fake.

"For the record," Des said. "You did good. Scary. Reckless. Going to give me gray hair before graduation. But good."

"Thanks, Des."

"Don't get used to me being nice," he said. "It's a limited engagement."

Kenzie Ochoa ran past us toward the buses, her backpack bouncing. She stopped, turned around, and ran backward for a few steps.

"Wren!" she yelled. "I still have it! It's been almost a month!"

She held up her retainer case like a trophy.

"Proud of you, Kenzie," I said.

"I know," she said, and turned and ran.

* * *

My dad made an actual dinner that night. Not the standing-at-the-counter kind. Roast chicken, potatoes, green beans from a can because neither of us is a hero. We ate at the table across from each other without either of us performing normal for the other one. I think it might have been the first time in six years that had happened.

He didn't ask about the case. I didn't bring up the photo. But when I got up to clear my plate, he caught my wrist, just for a second.

"I'm proud of you," he said. "I don't say that enough either."

He let go before either of us had to figure out what to do with how much that landed.

After dinner I found him in the front hall with a dish towel and a bottle of glass cleaner, standing in front of Rowan's picture. The green dress. The laugh at something off-camera.

He'd taken it down off the wall. I'd never seen it off the wall. There was a rectangle on the paint behind it, a shade lighter than the rest.

"I do it on Sundays," he said, without looking at me. "In case you were wondering. When you're at your run. Every Sunday."

"I know," I said. "I figured it out when I was eleven."

He almost smiled. He held the frame out to me.

I took it. I held my sister in my hands for the first time in six years. Not a phone. Not a screenshot. Her. I sprayed the glass and wiped it the way he showed me, in circles, then straight lines to get the streaks out, and handed it back.

He hung it up. He took a step back and tilted it a hair to the left, then a hair to the right, then left again.

"Dad," I said. "The photo. Under her bed. The torn one."

He went still. He kept looking at Rowan.

"I'm not going to ask tonight," I said. "I just want to say I'm going to ask. Sometime. And I want you to know I'm going to ask."

He didn't say anything for a long time. The kitchen clock ticked, four minutes fast.

"I'm not going to tell you I don't know," he said finally. "Not again. That's the only thing I can promise you right now." He folded the dish towel over his shoulder. "When you ask, I'm going to tell you something true. Even if it's *not yet*."

"Okay."

"Is that enough?"

I thought about Reyes on our porch steps. *I don't think anything's enough.*

"It's a lot," I said.

* * *

I went upstairs.

There's a drawer in my desk where I keep the birthday cards from my mother. Every few years, a different postmark. I've never opened one.

I opened the drawer and looked at them. A small stack of envelopes, my name on the front in handwriting I don't really remember.

I didn't open them. I want to be honest about that. I'm not there yet, and I'm not sure I'll ever be, and I'm not going to pretend some big thing happened that didn't.

But I looked at them. And I thought about the theory I've been running since I was ten. That people who look back at everything aren't the kind of people who leave.

I've looked back at a lot of things in the last six weeks. I've looked back at things I'd been keeping at almost two years for a reason. I went into the room I'd been staying out of. I asked the question. I followed it all the way to a bench at the edge of a cliff.

I don't think my mother ever did any of that.

I closed the drawer. Gently. I didn't throw anything away.
